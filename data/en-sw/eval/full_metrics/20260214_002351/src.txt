Ideology refers to ideas and beliefs that legitimize social issues. These beliefs legitimize political authority and the sets of actions that carry out practices.
Ideology gains authority and legitimacy voluntarily without the use of force. Political and religious leaders align themselves with certain ideologies to achieve their goals because they understand that these ideologies are accepted by members of society.
Change is inevitable, and as different sectors in society undergo change, leaders also adapt their leadership approaches. Likewise, as history changes, ideology also shifts within society.
The article has explained the concept of ideology, clarified the types of ideology, and identified them in the plays Maua kwenye Jua la Asubuhi (2004) and Kifo Kisimani (2001).
Some of the types of ideology identified in the selected plays include religious ideology, liberation ideology, governance ideology, cultural ideology, feudal ideology, and capitalist ideology. These types have been identified through the discourse of characters and the context of their conversations.
The research in this article is guided by the Critical Discourse Analysis (CDA) theory established by Norman Fairclough (1989). This theory explains the relationship that exists between political, discursive, cultural, textual, and social events.
It highlights the impact of ideology in these events. It also examines how such discourse influences these events in society.

INTRODUCTION Cultural ideology, feudal ideology, and capitalist ideology THEORY Ideology is a concept with many meanings. Eagleton (1991) has listed some descriptions of the concept of ideology as: the process of creating meaning, symbols, and values in society. Second, ideology is a collection of ideas held by a particular social group or class. Third, ideology consists of ideas or beliefs that legitimize political authority and the sets of actions that carry out practices. Eagleton (1991) further explains that ideology derives its meaning from historical changes. Marx and Engels (1977) define ideology as all human ideas and views regarding what they say and think about religion, law, politics, economics, and morals. Ideology guides how an author depicts themes and their intention. Ideology directs a person to perform good or bad actions (Murithi, 2017). Writing styles and methods have ideological effects, as noted by Fairclough (2001).
This article is guided by the theory of Critical Discourse Analysis (CDA). The CDA theory was founded by Norman Fairclough (1989) at the Lancaster School of Linguistics. Key proponents of this theory include Wodak (1997) and Dijk (1998). According to Wodak (1997), CDA focuses on ideology and governance based on written, visual, and spoken data. Wodak states that CDA regards language as a social act and considers the context of language to be very important. Fairclough (2001) explains that CDA is a crucial social science that examines the problems people face due to specific social circumstances and how to avoid those problems.
He further explains that the concept of discourse is guided by ideology.
The pillars of CDA that guided the research for this article are: ideology, culture, discourse, levels of analysis, and the structure of society.

The types of ideology identified in the selected plays include: religious ideology, liberation, governance, cultural ideology, feudal ideology, and capitalist ideology. The types of ideology listed are based on the general ideas of various scholars. Religious Ideology Davis (1994) describes religion as a social practice. Therefore, religion influences society by setting criteria for judging what humans do within society. He further explains that religion emphasizes that not everything a human thinks and does is good and valuable. Religious beliefs in society create controversy when interpreting worldly matters outside the realm of spiritual understanding. Religion shows that the social order was not established by humans, but by a being beyond them. This belief is passed down from one generation to another as part of culture.
Davis (1994) shows the use of political language and the misuse of religious language in politics.
Politicians use the religious system that guides humans to oppress others. He gives the example of the Israelites, who were granted the holy land by God. They used the belief of being given land by God to legitimize their actions and their situation, especially toward the Palestinians. Even Israelites who did not value the things of God used the notion of ‘being given land by God’ as a justification for themselves.
Religion has many meanings depending on the perspectives of various scholars. Cosmstock (1984) defines religion as that which is sacred, the commitment to promoting good deeds, love for humanity, and faith in deities. He goes on to explain that religion is what we practice unseen and which preserves society.

But what are the problems that define religious actions when they are being resolved?
According to Fitzgerald (2011), religion generally refers to a distinctive belief about human experience and institutions. He considers religion as matters of spirituality and the ‘other world’. It is a belief in supernatural powers.
Religion is love, peace, does not condone violence, and does not engage in politics.
Fitzgerald mentions some types of religion such as Christianity, Hinduism, Islam, and Buddhism. These religions are guided by different ideologies. According to various scholars, religion is a social practice involving spiritual matters and belief in a being greater than humans. Human actions are guided by the faith they have in this higher being. Plamenatz (1970), Davis (1994), and Cavanaugh (2009) regard religious ideology as a belief used to mislead human beings.
The social order established by a being superior to humans is used to conceal the inner nature of humans and to make them believe that they are victims of forces beyond their control.
The perspective of the playwright in Maua kwenye Jua la Asubuhi and Kifo Kisimani aligns with the ideas of Plamenatz, Davis, and Cavanaugh in the way he portrays the beliefs of the characters based on the context of dialogue and expressions guided by ideology. The playwright demonstrates how the characters perpetuate violence and oppress others on the basis of religious ideology. His perspective is that religion is used to sustain bad leadership and mislead members of society from liberating themselves from oppression.
According to Eddins (1972), liberation is a political ideology that provides guidance for the formulation of public policies. Liberators believe that the main goal of public policy is to protect the rights of the oppressed and ensure equality of growth and full self-development.
Furthermore, Gramsci (1994) explains that it is difficult to attain liberation. Those in need of liberation have been assigned roles by society ‘for the benefit of all’. They have become accustomed to these roles and view them as normal. Gramsci goes on to explain that members of society need to recognize and understand their place in history, their rights, and their responsibilities.
He emphasizes the importance of education in achieving liberation and the need for education in democracy. For him, education enables society to control the forces that govern them and to attain liberation, although it will take a long time to achieve liberation.
Liberation Ideology According to Montero (2009), liberation is a process that involves social transformation aimed at promoting equality, opposing oppression and injustice. Liberation also eliminates institutions that perpetuate these evils.
Montero (already cited) explains that liberation is directed towards social sectors that are oppressed.
Liberation aims to raise people's awareness of their rights and the importance of living in an equal and democratic society. Given the various interpretations of the concept of the ideology of liberation, it is clear that liberation addresses the rights of people who have been oppressed by their leaders. The goal of liberation is to give them freedom and the ability to advance themselves in all aspects of life. The ideology of liberation upholds the belief in freeing oneself from bad leadership. Groups that adhere to this belief implement this process by advancing revolutions against poor leadership.
Kitto (2019) explains that revolution is an important step to help society achieve development for the lower classes by removing the upper class from power and creating a classless society. Revolutionaries abolish the capitalist system and establish socialism.
Njogu (1997) explains that a revolution can occur if those in power do not take into account various social circumstances, either due to neglect or indifference.
Pavkovic (2005) describes the ideology of liberation as a set of beliefs, value judgments, and guidelines that direct political actions with the aim of liberating an oppressed group from their oppressors.
To control the oppressed, oppressors employ brutal methods. He goes on to explain that the oppressed use force and violence to free themselves from the cruelty of their oppressors.

Muya and Mwakagenda (2013) have defined the concept of governance as the exercise of political, economic, or other forms of authority in managing a country's affairs at all levels. Governance can be good or bad.
They define good governance as the exercise of authority characterized by transparency, public participation, accountability, productivity, efficiency, integrity, and equity.
Waito’s ideas regarding the use of demonstrations align with Pavkovic’s (2005) view that, in order to liberate oneself from the oppression of persecutors, power and violence are employed.

Governance Ideology According to Tangus and Sang (2020), governance is a system that defines the structures used by organizations or leaders.
It is a system that establishes principles for the protection of rights and has responsibilities to ensure that the management of all societal affairs is effective. They go on to explain that it is important for the governance system to address all levels of society, politics, and the economy. Based on the various definitions of the concept of governance, it is clear that governance ideology involves the exercise of authority to improve and strengthen all social, political, and economic spheres. Leaders are required to promote good governance that advocates for and protects the rights of community members and ensures equity in the distribution of community resources.

Ideology of Culture There is a strong relationship between discourse, culture, and society according to Fairclough (1989). Discourse embodies the culture of the particular society.
The playwright of "Maua kwenye Jua la Asubuhi" (2004) has depicted the issue of culture through the lineage that arises from marriage.
The discussions of bride price and marriage mentioned are cultural issues within the African community.
According to Nsereko (1975), paying bride price is a custom in which a man is required to give property to the wife's family before marriage.
This is a gesture of gratitude to those who took care of the wife.
Dowry strengthens marriage and legitimizes the union between the families involved.
Some of the phrases used in the selected play to indicate this culture are: marriage, dowry, and wedding.

When the Arabs arrived on the East African coast, they encountered the Swahili, and it was then that Swahili borrowed words and sounds from Arabic that did not previously exist in the Swahili language. This article investigates and identifies the etymology of certain Arabic words found in the Swahili language and examines how Arabic sounds were borrowed, altered, and adapted into Swahili. Furthermore, it explores how the original meanings of some words changed after being borrowed from Arabic.

Sound changes in borrowed Arabic words. Many Arabic words borrowed into the Swahili language, in addition to undergoing changes to adapt vowel patterns and conform to the structure of Swahili words, also experienced alterations to their original sounds. These sounds were changed because they did not exist at all in Swahili, and even if they had been borrowed directly, they would have caused pronunciation difficulties for Swahili speakers who were not accustomed to articulating these Arabic sounds. The following examples illustrate the changes in pronunciation of some Arabic words that have been incorporated into the Swahili language.
Differentiation Between the Arabic ي (Yee) and the Swahili Vowel. The Arabic ي (Yee) or /y/ is the final consonant in the Arabic alphabet.
There are some Arabic words that have a (yee) with a shadda.
For example, MANIYYI منێ, NABIYYI نبێ, HAYYU حێ, TWAYYIB ﻄﯿﺐ, and MAYYITU مێټ. When these words were borrowed into Swahili, the ي (yee) was dropped and replaced by the vowel /i/.
MANIYYI became MANII, NABIYYI became NABII.
HAYYU became HAI, TWAYYIB became taib, MAYYITU became MAITI, etc. Differentiation of the Arabic ع (‘Ain) and the Swahili Vowel ﻉ (‘ain) is the eighteenth consonant in the Arabic alphabet. For example: AR’LLI ﺃﺮﺾ (VCCV) becomes ARDHI (VCCV), KHAMSIIN ﺨﻤﺴﯿﻦ (CVCVCCVC) — when this word was borrowed into Swahili, it retained its original Arabic structure, but underwent a few changes, namely, /kh/ changed to /h/, a vowel immediately after /s/ was dropped, and a vowel was added to the end of the word. But no vowel was inserted after /m/, so the Arabic structure was preserved as it entered Swahili and the word became HAMSINI (CVCVCVCV). Changes in meaning for some of the borrowed Arabic words. Most Arabic words borrowed and incorporated into Swahili retained their original meaning from Arabic. However, some words changed meaning after being borrowed and acquired new meanings in the Swahili language. This section will examine some words whose meanings changed following borrowing. Dari is a Swahili word borrowed from Arabic ﺪﺍﺭ (dār). The original Arabic meaning of this word is HOUSE (NYUMBA), as seen in the phrase ﺪﺍﺭﺴﻼﻡ DĀR SALĀM meaning HOUSE OF PEACE. When borrowed into Swahili, however, its meaning changed—it no longer referred to a 'house' but rather to the upper part of a house, just below the roof. Alhamisi is a Swahili word borrowed from the Arabic word ﺃﻠﺧﻤﻴﺱ (alkhamīs). According to the Arabic weekly calendar, Alkhamiis is the fifth day. But in Swahili, it is the sixth day of the week. Swahili borrowed two words, ALHAMISI and IJUMAA, from Arabic. However, the word ALHAMISI—which meant ‘the fifth day’ in Arabic—when it entered the Swahili language, its meaning changed to ‘the sixth day of the week.’ Sakafu is a Swahili word borrowed from the Arabic ﺴﻘﻒ (saqf). In Arabic, saqf means CEILING, that is, the upper part of a house beneath the roof. However, when this word was borrowed into Swahili, its meaning changed to refer to the lower part of the house—that which is laid and cemented. Nahau is another word borrowed from the Arabic ﺍﻟنﺤﻭ (an-nahwu), which originally means GRAMMAR in Arabic. However, when incorporated into Swahili, the word took on a new meaning: a group of words that together carry a special meaning not directly derived from the usual meanings of the individual words.
Linguistics is a word that was also borrowed from the Arabic language ﺍﻹﺴﻡ (al-ismu), whose original meaning is NAME. When this word was borrowed and incorporated into Swahili, its meaning changed to refer to the discipline that studies and scientifically analyzes language.
Waraka was also borrowed from the Arabic language ﻮﺭﻗﺔ (waraqatu). The original meaning of this word in Arabic is LEAF OF A TREE.
However, when it was borrowed and incorporated into the Swahili language, its meaning changed to refer to documents written on a piece of paper that convey certain information. In conclusion, this article examines and analyzes the various phonological changes of Arabic words that were borrowed and incorporated into Swahili.
After identifying various changes, it was determined that many Arabic words were adapted into Swahili after being borrowed, with their consonants altered to simplify pronunciation for Bantu speakers. In addition, there are sounds and pronunciation patterns that were borrowed directly from Arabic without any modification. Moreover, there were also changes in meaning in some of the borrowed Arabic words incorporated into the Swahili language. Therefore, the evidence shows that both changes in meaning and pronunciation occurred in some Swahili words borrowed from Arabic.

1.1 The Theory of Formalism Schmitz (2007) and Carter (2006) have outlined several ideas of formalist theorists as follows: distinguishing between the use of ordinary language and literary language, they believed and emphasized that the concept of innovation or novelty is the main function of art, and they preferred literary criticism to have a scientific orientation; thus, they sought to establish scientific approaches in literature. According to the ideas of the formalists, all literary works utilize this theory because a work cannot be considered literary if it does not possess artistic qualities. These artistic qualities involve the construction of language elements that demonstrate the novelty of the work. Therefore, anyone who critiques any literary work based on the theory of Formalism will inevitably have to confront the issue of "novelty"—that is, the features that construct the use of language are unavoidable. The theory of Formalism is among the theories that analyze and explain the use of language in the disciplines of literature and linguistics. This theory originated from activists in two academic circles in Russia: on the one hand, the linguists of the Moscow Linguistics Circle, and on the other, the Society for the Study of Poetic Language (OPAYAZ), both aiming to bring revolutions to linguistics and literary criticism (Schmitz, 2007).
In conclusion, the theory of Formalism has greatly contributed to the advancement and development of literary criticism. This contribution stems from the concept of innovation or novelty in literary works, the challenges and various debates it has sparked, which have generated diverse knowledge and ideas within the field of literature, as well as the emergence of other theories such as the theory of Modernism.

While Bahati is deeply immersed in love, unaware of herself and her surroundings, she realizes that Idi does not truly love her, but instead loves only himself, and that she, Bahati, is merely an instrument to fulfill his worldly desires.
Like a bird caught in a snare, her efforts to find escape only lead her closer to ruin.

5.1 Literary Techniques in the Swahili Novel 5.1.1 The Use of the Letter Technique A letter is an ancient means of communication between an individual and another individual, an individual and a group, an institution and an individual, or between institutions. Its aim is to convey information or a particular message that is important for the intended recipient to know. Idi received the letter with surprise because he was not expecting it.
He turned the letter over and saw Bahati’s name written in hurried handwriting. He opened it and read: Idi, Don’t be surprised when I tell you that right now I am in bed with Mr. Mwinyi, and we are planning the day for our real wedding.
It is not permitted to interfere with us because we have our own free will.
I warn you for your own safety, that your plans should cease once you have received this letter.
Send my regards to so-and-so … From the depths of my heart, Bahati.
The letter ignited a fire in Idi’s heart and boiled him with anger. It struck him like a sudden, fierce storm.
Suddenly his eyes changed color, his forehead became furrowed, and his jaw dropped, in a display of intense anguish.
He read that letter again, and the more he read it, the angrier he became.

This letter was written by Bahati to Idi, her lover whom she loves very much, but here she is informing him that she is now in love with another person named Mwinyi.
Mwinyi is Idi's boss, and Idi wanted Bahati to deceive Mwinyi by making him believe she loved him so that they could get money, while in reality, the one she truly loved was Idi. Now, it is truly shocking to see that the love between Mwinyi and Bahati has become genuine and not a joke as Idi had expected.
Idi is now filled with great doubt about whether the wealth he hoped to gain from Mwinyi through Bahati will actually materialize, or if it has already been lost.
The theme of love, as well as that of theft and smuggling, are greatly developed through the use of the letter mentioned above.
Bahati is weighing whether Idi truly loves her, while Idi grows angry as his plan to gain Mwinyi's wealth through Bahati slips away.
Also, through the above letter, the author develops the theme that not everything can turn out as one expects.
Planning and implementation of something are two different things, although they are interdependent (Scott, 1995).
Idi did not expect that telling Bahati to pretend to love Mwinyi so they could gain wealth would actually make Bahati truly fall in love with Mwinyi and leave him.
Here, the theme of warnings is developed, emphasizing that love should not be experimented with, as at any moment it can change and become completely different from what was originally intended.

5.1.2 The Use of Proverbs in Kiu Proverbs are expressions that are similar to and resemble sayings used by human beings as tools of communication (Msokile, 1992).
However, proverbs often do not have two sides as is the case with sayings.
A proverb, on its part, has two sides, where one side serves as a question and the other side provides the answer to the question posed (Wamitila, 2003).
Proverbs are sayings that arise from expressions whose nature is that they emerge and disappear, and those that do not disappear but endure are what are called proverbs.
Proverbs are a literary technique used by authors of literary works to underscore or emphasize a particular theme they want their audience to grasp.
In his novel *Kiu*, Mohamed Suleiman has used proverbs in his work as follows: Things were not good for Idi, as his intention was not to truly love her but merely to toy with her.
Most responsible parents know that when young people engage in romantic relationships before marriage, it often leads to them toying with each other and the issue of marriage gets lost. Therefore, as a parent, Cheusi wanted her daughter to end things with Idi immediately.
The proverb used in the above passage has been employed to build and emphasize the theme of a mother's guidance to her child.
Sociologists explain that habit is something that shapes character, and habits always persist (Ritzer, 2008).
If in society girls and boys are left free to have relationships before marriage, they will consider it acceptable, and the issue of getting married will no longer hold any significance for them. Parents must fulfill their responsibilities properly so that members of society, especially the youth, see that having relationships before marriage is a bad thing that should never be encouraged.
5.1.3 The Use of Metaphor in Kiu A metaphor is a figure of speech that is used to compare two things with different characteristics without using any linking word of comparison, as is the case with simile (Kyando, 2013). The things being compared are considered to be entirely alike, without any differences.
For example, one might say, “Mwajuma is a little rabbit hiding in a tree.” Here, Mwajuma is being compared to a rabbit as if they are two things that are exactly alike, without any difference at all.
The main purpose of using metaphors in literary works is to emphasize the theme being presented to the reader.
Mohamed Suleiman used a metaphor when he said: When people said that no one had ever outsmarted Mwinyi, their words were not exaggerated. Mwinyi had enough money to buy himself most of the things that a sixty-two-year-old man like him could desire, except for just one thing.
That is what made him feel as though his entire life had been wasted, and that his wealth was like a rice cake that had been spoiled with sand (p.01).
The words emphasized in ink in the phrase here express a saying that something good has now turned bad, something sweet has now become bitter, and something valuable has now lost its value.
Mr. Mwinyi was a wealthy man who possessed all kinds of riches, but he longed to have a romantic relationship with a young woman named Bahati, whom he had come to love deeply. Every approach he tried failed to win Bahati over, leading him to complain that “his wealth was like a cake mixed with sand,” meaning his wealth did not help him at all and was of no benefit to him.
Essentially, a rice cake that has sand mixed into it becomes inedible and loses all its flavor because the eater fears their teeth will bite into the sand.
This saying has indeed been used to emphasize the theme of love—that if a person is wealthy in every material sense but lacks the love they long for, then their wealth becomes meaningless and causes them to live with more hardship and mental anguish than even a poor person.
Sociologists acknowledge that there are certain things which are generally considered highly desirable to possess, such as wealth (Cross, 2011).
However, there comes a time when a person lacks something they thought was minor, and their wealth does not help them obtain it, even though they believed their riches were the key to getting everything.
This is akin to saying that the rice cake now has sand in it, for all of Mwinyi’s wealth could not enable him to win Bahati’s love.
Another use of the saying appears when Cheusi, who is Bahati’s mother, was warning her daughter about her relationship with Idi.
He says: There was a brief but bitter silence.
Then Cheusi continued to advise her: "That Idi of yours, let me tell you, he doesn't want you, he has nothing.
Whoever truly desires a woman marries her. He just wants to toy with you and then discard you, moving on while laughing at you.
I have already realized that just because you have studied your English you think I am ignorant, well then … One day you will cry like a dog, mouth raised, and I will be here (pp. 25–26).” In this passage the writer uses the saying, “you will cry like a dog, mouth raised,” to refer to the theme of guidance and warnings given by a mother to her child. Cheusi was warning her daughter Bahati about the relationship she had started with At that moment, Iddi, her driver, entered without knocking. Mwinyi was startled and asked him, “Why are you late?” Iddi wiped his sweat to show the effort he had made, shook his head sadly, and said, “I found the door locked.” Those words broke Mwinyi’s heart.
He slumped his shoulders and shrank into the chair, feeling that life was being unfair to him.
Waiting without end—six months had passed, and relentless yearning weighed heavily upon him. He had already endured enough; yet he still did not know when the day of relief would come.
But ah, her late mother’s words: “Whatever comes your way, accept it” (p. 02).
The use of metaphor in the above phrase shows that words are compared to a knife or anything sharp that is capable of cutting something.
“Those words struck Mwinyi to the core,” is a statement that shows Mwinyi’s hope of winning Bahati had vanished as soon as he received information from Idi that he could not find Bahati and that Bahati’s door was locked.
Sociologists explain that a person may reach a point where they lose hope of obtaining something they have long desired but are unable to acquire (Webster, 2006).
Losing hope in life is a bad thing because it can lead a person to do something harmful that may even endanger others. For example, Mwinyi's despair at not being able to have Bahati could drive him to make the decision to commit a crime such as rape.
The act of rape is terrible for the victim but also for the perpetrator, as it can result in spending the rest of their life in prison.
For a sixty-two-year-old man like Mwinyi to be imprisoned is a terrible thing that could cause him to die in prison.

Things such as trees, words, insects, animals, and birds are depicted as performing human actions even though they do not have the ability to do so. The use of personification in literary works is intended to build the theme in an imaginative way so that the reader reflects a little in order to grasp the intended theme of the author or narrator of the work before them.
In the novel *Kiu*, there is the use of personification applied for this very purpose, as manifested in the following excerpt: “People say that love never meets, but why have yours and Bahati’s?” Bahati cried out with anguish and sorrow, until even sleep took pity on her, swiftly overtook her, and rested her on the bed.

The words emphasized in the above passage are used as personification, because in real life only a human being is capable of showing compassion toward another person or creature. Sleep is a state in which a person loses consciousness, as expressed in the Swahili saying, "sleep is half of death." However, in the above passage, sleep appears to have been given the ability to feel compassion, as if it were human.
5.1.5 The Use of Suspense in Kiu Suspense is a literary technique used by authors of literary works to craft their works in such a way that they effectively convey their intended themes to the target audience.
This is a technique of delaying the reader from obtaining the solution or the truth of the matter, and making them continue reading until they encounter a solution or some explanation that will provide the answer they intended to find (Senkoro, 2006).
The suspense technique is deliberately used by authors of literary works to give the reader or listener the desire to continue reading, especially when the work in question is lengthy enough to tire the reader. The length of the work will seem very short if there are suspenseful moments that captivate the reader.
Suspense is an ancient literary technique that is widely used in oral literature and even in written literature. Mohamed Suleiman, in his novel *Kiu*, has employed this technique in the way he begins narrating his story. For example: That evening, he was stretched out on a reclining chair, his eyes fixed on the ceiling, counting the minutes. His desire was not great, and the anxiety of failure had robbed him of joy. If only what he wanted was within his grasp, he would seize it and quench his thirst.
If what he wanted could be bought with money, he would surely have scraped together every cent he had.
But the thing itself depended on the will of another person, someone with taste and pride.
So he had no choice but to ask and wait (pp. 01–02).
This passage contains a sense of anxiety, as there is something that makes Mwinyi uncomfortable and torments him despite being wealthy.
This is not stated explicitly, but it is merely mentioned that if obtaining that thing required money, Mwinyi would provide it. “Stop joking, Iddi,” Mwinyi scolded him.
“Do you think a child like that could love an old man like me (p.05)?” In this passage, the tension built by the author on the first page of his novel begins to find answers on page five.
This is where the issue that troubles Mwinyi is mentioned, to the extent that he lacks peace despite all his great wealth.
That thing is none other than the love he had for a girl known by the name Bahati. Mwinyi was a man of sixty-two years, but he loved Bahati very much, even though she was a young girl who had not yet finished school.
In this context, another tension arises: could Bahati agree to become the lover of an older man, just as Mwinyi did? This too creates suspense for the readers, who will be compelled to read further to find out what will happen regarding Mwinyi’s love for the young girl Bahati.
The use of this technique of suspense has been employed with the aim of enhancing and developing the theme of love in the novel Kiu.
This technique appears to be used repeatedly, as whenever the answer or solution to the initial suspense is found, another suspense is introduced (Mlacha, 1991).
For example, in the excerpt above we find the solution that what had been troubling Mwinyi Raha and causing him sleepless nights was his love for Bahati's daughter.
After realizing this, another anxiety has arisen as to whether such a young girl like Bahati could agree to be in a relationship with old Mwinyi, who could even be Bahati's father.
Readers will continue reading until they find a solution, and after that, they will encounter another anxiety.
Conclusion The specific objective of this work was to analyze and describe the stylistic techniques used to construct social themes in the novel "Kiu" by Mohamed Suleiman. Accordingly, the findings include the use of stylistic techniques such as letters, expressions, metaphors, personification, and anxiety.
The research findings show that the novel 'Kiu' by Mohamed Suleiman is rich in stylistic techniques, but in this study we have focused only on the use of the letter-writing technique, sayings, metaphor, anxiety, and suspense. The main reason for selecting these five techniques is that they adequately meet the objectives of this research.
The use of the letter-writing technique was employed with the aim of developing and advancing the theme of love, in which Bahati wanted to know if Iddi truly loved her. Bahati wrote a letter to inform Iddi that she was now genuinely in love with Mwinyi and asked Iddi not to bother her, as they were now planning their wedding. This letter tormented Iddi to the point where he did not know what to do, because he had already realized that all the plans he had made with Bahati were now ruined. However, the content of the letter was intended to test whether Iddi truly loved Bahati.
Bahati knew that Iddi loved her because he became upset after reading the letter she had sent him.

Intertextuality is a method, skill, or discipline and a guide for the scientific analysis of texts, especially artistic and non-artistic works, to determine the presence of influence through borrowing or dialogue; that is, the similarity in form, content, and technique between one text and another. This resemblance can be direct or involve proximity. Such interaction is also referred to as an echo.
It is believed that intertextuality is among the oldest compositional techniques; however, as a critical approach, it still appears to be unfamiliar and has not been extensively addressed in the field of Kiswahili literature and art. For this reason, this article will contribute to the Kiswahili body of work dealing with criticism through the theory of intertextuality, in order to identify the intertextuality present in texts, for example in the play Our Cohesion Our Triumph.
The results of the analysis have revealed a considerable presence of intertextuality connected to the author’s upbringing and development in religious (spiritual), physical (cultural and moral), social, and academic dimensions – in one way or another. It is part of these findings that constitutes this article.
Therefore, this article presents the intertextuality between the selected play Our Cohesion Our Triumph and writings from sacred books, allegory in the selection and creation of characters, philosophical echoes, chapter or plot titles, and the mythology of symbols.

Allegory is an artistic device used by a writer to choose a name that reflects the character, behavior, and experiences of a character in the work they are creating. For example, a writer may name their character “Hasidi” (The Envious One). To justify this name, the writer must develop that character to act with envy from beginning to end. The selection and creation of characters whose traits align with their names is the artistic technique known as allegory.
Normally, these characters possess the traits of flat characters (those who do not undergo changes in character) in order to justify their names, because if they were to change in character, their names would no longer correspond to their new traits. Therefore, in terms of character and stance, these characters remain unchanged from the beginning to the end of the play. Mlacha and Madumulla (1991) describe such characters as ones who do not appear to grow in character, actions, or psychologically.
The main goal of this approach is to persuade the public to embrace good deeds and names that embody goodness, so as to reject evil and oppose the use of names that carry negative meanings, since African societies believe that a name shapes the character of the individual.

Authors of the modern Swahili novel convey messages to the audience by focusing on experimental literary techniques.
Intertextuality is one of the key elements of those experimental techniques.
The novels themselves have been called “experimental novels” or “modern novels.”
Other experts have classified them as “Twenty-First Century novels.”
The author Kyallo Wadi Wamitila is one of the modern writers who employs this new writing style to address various issues affecting society.
The theory of postmodernism has guided this research in the analysis of the aspect of intertextuality. Various types of texts referenced in the presentation of the theme of violence were identified and explained.
The novel Msichana wa Mbalamwezi was deliberately selected because it is presented through numerous short narratives that reference different texts.
The descriptive content analysis method was used to analyze the data related to the overall issue of intertextuality. The data was analyzed by focusing on themes, settings, and key elements of experimental writing techniques. The findings were presented descriptively.
It was revealed that a lack of order in the conduct of affairs is the true reflection of today's society.
The referenced texts have shown that the ordinary citizen has been oppressed and provided with poor services by the leaders.
This article has examined and evaluated how the author uses the technique of intertextuality as a device for presenting the theme of oppression in the novel Msichana wa Mbalamwezi.

Instead of adhering to the realist framework, the author Kyallo Wadi Wamitila, as a modern writer, has written this novel based on new realism.
Rettova (2016) has described Wamitila as a writer who leans towards modernism because he values the use of experimental techniques.
Intertextuality is a key element in the perspective of modernist writing.
Scholars have noted that it is difficult to draw boundaries between modernity and modernism. Modernity refers to a period in the history of literature that was closely tied to changes in the arts, especially between the 1880s and 1930s in Europe.
At that time, human beings demonstrated their ability and power to create, strengthen, and shape their environment through new scientific, technological, and experimental methods (Best et al., 2001).
These changes arose from the fact that, in the past, literature was dominated by religion and politics. The artistic changes involved literary genres such as poetry, drama, novels, dance, and film.
Core themes, authentic language, realism, and a coherent structure are the key aspects that have been emphasized.
European cities that witnessed these changes include Paris, Vienna, Rome, London, and Moscow. These changes were called Modernity and were the core and foundation of modernism.
Abrams (1993) maintains that modernity is used to describe and identify new features of themes, genres, concepts, and literary styles associated with the first decades of the twentieth century, that is, after the Second World War. Abrams further emphasizes that the characteristics referred to vary depending on the critics involved.
Wamitila (2007) states, “Despite these differences, most critics agree that the foundation of these features is a departure from the traditional foundations of art and Western or pre-existing culture.”
According to this, it is evident that modernity completely broke away from the conventions of literary composition that were followed before the twentieth century. Although the exact time of the emergence of modernity and modernism is a matter of debate, it is clear that modernism arose and was built on the foundations of modernity. Modernism emerged and was established in the twentieth century.
It was associated with Nietzsche, Heidegger, Derrida, Lyotard, Baudrillard, among others.
Other scholars who have contributed to the theory of modernism include Jurgen Habermas (1987), who emphasizes that the contemporary world relies more on aspects of modernism, in which all activities related to modern human beings are examined through multiple narratives.
A recent interpreter of this theory is Fredric Jameson (1991). This article focuses on various views of the theory as established by Jean-Francois Lyotard (1984), Jean Baudrillard (1994), Fredric Jameson (1991), and others.
Lyotard (1984) maintains the controversial theory that the consciousness of members of society has been greatly transformed by the changes brought about by the industrial development period. This situation has led society to change its cultures and embrace modernist cultures.
The modernist period disrupted the boundaries established by traditional culture. This phenomenon has manifested in various aspects of society, including literature.
Life and people are not orderly, and therefore literary works should not have any particular order. Modernism is closely associated with popular culture, where people do things in whatever way they wish without following traditional norms.
The writer gives himself permission to use another artist’s work to create a new one.

This is to say that, along with popular literature, modernism is also associated with popular performance.
One well-known feature in popular literature is the “stream of consciousness,” which is a narrative technique in prose writing that employs lengthy descriptions focusing on the thoughts passing through a character’s mind (Wamitila, 2006).
This performance takes place anywhere and does not follow any particular order. Often, the writer’s main goal is simply to entertain society.
Popular performance uses popular language.
Since language is an important tool in literary writing, modernism focuses on the use of language in a very free manner without adhering to its fluency or standardization. 1.4 Intertextuality Intertextuality is the translation of the English term “intertextuality,” which was first used by the French philosopher Julia Kristeva in her book ‘Dialogue and Novel’ (1966). According to Kristeva, every word is a reference to other words. Thus, emphasis is placed on the idea that texts are related to and depend on one another to create meaning.
Stam (2000), as quoted in Anderson (2004:1), states, “Any emerging text must inevitably coexist with another, or with other texts with which the original has also coexisted.”
This statement demonstrates that a literary work does not originate solely from the mind of the author or from a vacuum, but is instead a collection of different texts.
(Anderson, 2004) claims that intertextuality involves “that which has already been said.” Intertextuality is an important element of modernism, in which Wamitila writes.
In addition, intertextuality is one of the key elements of experimental writing. The intertextuality that frequently appears in the novel Msichana wa Mbalamwezi is characterized by the use of short stories, branches of oral literature, philosophical opinions, poetry/songs, and language mixing.
All these texts have been used in this novel to present the theme of oppression in various spheres of society such as health, education, and love. All these aspects interact and depend on each other in developing the creativity of the author.
According to Julia Kristeva (1980), a text is related to other texts.
This article has established that in presenting the theme of oppression in the novel Msichana wa Mbalamwezi, various texts are interconnected, interact, and depend on each other.
Jilala (2016) explains that the meaning of one text is derived from other texts.
In literature, meaning is represented by themes (Mohammed, 1995). Intertextuality in the selected novel has manifested at various levels such as form, characterization, motif, and themes.
This article has identified the types of texts referenced in presenting the theme of oppression. Enani (2005) asserts that intertextuality influences the way a new text is read.
The novel Msichana wa Mbalamwezi has demonstrated the presence of stylistic intermingling. Many of the references in this novel are of different styles and have influenced the narrative structure and plot.
The audience is taken here and there. The reader starts reading one segment of the story, and before finishing it, another begins. This phenomenon results in complexity within the story itself.
Wamitila (2010) asserts that this type of reference can lead to either negative or positive outcomes.
Bakhtin (1981:271) argues that what is said is more than just the utterances themselves.
Different contexts affect literary works in various ways. Contexts such as economic, social, or political factors influence certain relationships within literary works (Wamitila, 2010).
Accordingly, this article has analyzed and critiqued the novel Msichana wa Mbalamwezi, identifying the different types of contexts referenced, their relationships, and how those relationships contribute to presenting the theme of oppression.

The author has used short narratives with specific intertextual references to illustrate how members of the community are denied their rights.

The current Swahili novel is appropriate because it serves as a true reflection of today’s society. It has become evident that short narratives referring to different texts are effective in presenting themes, rather than using a single long story written in the style of realism.
Any creator has the freedom to try new methods in carrying out their work without being restricted or fixated on traditional rules. Setting is defined as the time and place in which events, incidents, or actions occur in a given literary work. Settings can be real or imagined.
The study that informed this article aimed to examine the depiction of setting in Mohamed S. Mohamed's novel *Kiu* and Ken Walibora's novel *Kidagaa Kimemwozea*. Although the research had three specific objectives, this article focuses on just one objective—to assess the contribution of setting to the construction of character traits in the novels *Kiu* and *Kidagaa Kimemwozea*.
The assessment itself is grounded in a combination of two theories—Realism and Structuralism. The theory of Realism examines the authenticity of the social setting, while Structuralism emphasizes the function and interrelationship of artistic elements.
This evaluative study was conducted in the library, employing an analytical design. The research area is written literature, specifically the genre of the Swahili novel. The target population of the study comprises 10 realist novels that depict authentic settings.
A sample of two novels, *Kiu* and *Kidagaa Kimemwozea*, was selected through purposive sampling.
These novels were selected based on the abundance of the aspects under investigation, namely the depiction of setting. In addition to reflecting the objectives of the study, the composition of the selected novels aligns with the framework of the combined theoretical approaches. The primary data for the study was collected through intensive reading of the texts of the selected novels.
Secondary data was collected through intensive reading of books, theses, and various academic articles. The collected data was categorized according to the theoretical frameworks and objectives of the study.
They were analyzed according to their content and the results presented descriptively. The findings of the study indicate that settings have a direct contribution to the development of characters' traits.
These settings include: spatial settings (such as the banks of the Kiberenge River, the room of Chief Nasaba Bora, the forest, the cell, Mzee Mwinyi's room, and neighborhoods such as Vibyongoni, Selea, Ukele, and others) and temporal settings (the colonial era, five-year periods, the Second World War, and so on).
These settings were skillfully crafted in a way that greatly facilitated the development of the characters' traits.
The findings of this study are beneficial to novelists and literary critics in general. They will help them craft stories that portray and reflect authentic settings. Furthermore, literary critics will be enabled to identify and evaluate various settings along with their roles in literary works.

INTRODUCTION A literary work is constructed using various artistic elements, including the use of setting. This element is important in literary writing and contributes to the construction and delivery of the author's message. Setting is often used by a writer to convey their message to the reader. The element of setting has been defined by various scholars.
For example, Wamitila (2008) defines setting as an element that reveals to the reader where and when certain incidents or events took place. Settings can be realistic or imaginary. Realistic settings pertain to locations that exist in the real world. For instance, settings such as a forest, at the university, at home, in the office, in the city, and in places of worship. Imaginary settings refer to unusual environments and many are encountered through dreams. Examples of imaginary settings include paradise, the underworld, and hell.
The setting depicted for the reader remains in their mind. Many writers, when crafting their works, place them in different settings to satisfy various literary and artistic purposes. It is therefore valid to state that setting is a fundamental pillar in artistic compositions.
Setting influences the reader's perception in many ways, including providing a variety of images of what the author intended to convey (Wamitila, 2003; Matei, 2012; Massoud, 2022). These images can be, for example: visual imagery, which gives the reader the ability to 'see' what the author illustrates through words; movement imagery, which helps the reader experience an event 'in motion' according to the setting in which the event takes place; sensory imagery, where the reader experiences various feelings of the characters within the context as the author explores those emotions; gustatory imagery, which the reader perceives in settings where actions evoke a salivating response; and many others such as auditory imagery.
Taking the example of the area of Selea, which appears in the novel *Kiu*, the author tells us that it was a place (setting) characterized by pervasive poverty and numerous hardships; the residents lived in shacks and some in rooms plastered with mud, and the road to get there was so bad that even Mwinyi’s car could not reach it.
Characters such as Bahati and Chausiku’s mother, who lived in this neighborhood, were also poor. This means that the environment had a direct impact on these characters and their traits. They were poor, which is why they lived there.
Wamitila (2002) explains that setting informs the reader about the character's journey from beginning to end in the story. Therefore, the reader comes to understand the character in every location and at every stage of their development.
A good example is the character Mwalimu Majisfu in the novel *Kidagaa Kimemwozea*, about whom we are told of his childhood, his studies abroad, his involvement in alcoholism and failure to attend his classes at Nasaba Bora school, being dismissed from work and finding another job, becoming involved in theft of authors’ manuscripts, and failing to deliver lectures when invited to Wangwani. Here, we are given several traits of this character through various settings—that he was educated, an alcoholic, an academic thief, and negligent in his work.
Character traits often help to identify and distinguish a particular character easily. These traits frequently reveal their habits, behavior, or even personality within the given literary work.
In this regard, setting plays a significant role in depicting character traits (Wamitila, 2002). To a large extent, characters are influenced by the settings in which they are portrayed. Some literary works depict settings of poverty, numerous hardships, and characters living in shacks and mud-plastered rooms, as well as transportation that relies on poor roads. This means that the settings depicted by the artist have a direct impact on the construction of the traits of the characters in the given work.
This is the issue that was investigated in the research that produced this article. The main objective was to examine the depiction of setting in the novels *Kiu* and *Kidagaa Kimemwozea* in order to establish the relationship between setting and character traits. Through assessment, the argument advanced by this article is that the settings in which characters find themselves in the novels *Kiu* and *Kidagaa Kimemwozea* contribute to the construction of their traits. It has been established that setting also influences the way certain characters are depicted. Setting plays a significant role in endowing characters with particular traits (Wamitila, 2002). To a certain extent, the place and time in which a character finds themselves signals and affects the depiction of their traits. Therefore, this article explores the relationship between setting and character traits in these two works and clearly reveals the existence of this relationship as well as how these authors have portrayed their characters. The objectives of the study were guided by the combination of two theories, namely, the theory of Realism and the theory of Structuralism.
The theory of Realism emerged in the nineteenth century (Ntarangwi, 2004).
The term 'uhalisia' is a translation of the English word 'realism', which was originally used to refer to a branch of philosophy concerned with matters related to human perception.
Currently, this term is used to refer to two meanings: the first is one that evaluates the state of life, that is, the reality in literature which signifies the skill of composition and describes events according to objectivity; and the second meaning is the actual condition of human life.
The theory of Structuralism is often associated with the Prague linguists (Mwangi, 2005). It emphasizes the elements of an artistic work such as plot, message, characters, context, language, and how these interact to complete the work in question. This theory examines the relationships among the various components of literary compositions, such as structural narrative analysis. The theory focuses on the narration of an artistic work. For instance, structural analysis of a novel considers the novel as a self-contained work possessing structural elements such as plot, characters, and various narrative techniques that interrelate and depend on each other.

This article analyzes Swahili compound nouns that violate the Parenthesis Deletion Rule. This rule outlines the procedure for constructing a complete word using morphemes. Parentheses in this rule indicate boundaries and are deleted to signify that word formation rules do not access the structure of words formed at previous levels.
This study was conducted after it was observed that some Swahili compound nouns violate the Parenthesis Deletion Rule, especially when changes occur in the middle of the word after various morphemes are combined. The following compound noun illustrates the changes that happen in the middle of a word after various morphemes are joined: [mw [enyekiti]] → [we [enyeviti]]. In this study, we collected data from the Kamusi Kuu ya Kiswahili (2015), where thirty compound nouns that violate the Parenthesis Deletion Rule were used as primary data.
These nouns were selected based on the distinctive features of violating the Parenthesis Deletion Rule (PDR).
This study was guided by one pillar of the Lexical Morphology theory established by Kiparsky (1982) and developed by Katamba and Stonham (2019): the Parenthesis Deletion Rule.
The theory of Lexical Morphology emerged to compensate for Chomsky's Generative Grammar perspective, which did not recognize morphology as a distinct level of language. Essentially, this theory demonstrates the relationship between the rules that form morphological structures and the rules that govern how those structures are pronounced.
The data related to the research topic was collected from the library. Some of the works used in this study include previous dissertations, online articles, and journals. This research was guided by purposive sampling to identify relevant compound nouns with distinctive features. The findings of this study were presented through charts, tables, and explanations. This research contributes to linguistics, particularly by understanding and explaining compounding strategies in the Swahili language.

The topic of this article falls within the general area of morphology. This is a branch of linguistics that deals with the structure of words (Habwe and Karanja, 2004). Haspelmath and Sims (2013) define morphology as a branch of linguistics that examines the internal structure of words. Based on these definitions, we agree with these scholars' views that morphology is a branch of linguistics concerned with the structure of words.
Morphology as a discipline is divided into two main branches: inflectional morphology and word formation morphology. Inflectional morphology deals with the process of inflection in words, where inflectional affixes are added to word roots to represent various meanings, but these inflections do not change the word class. Word formation morphology is the field that addresses the linguistic processes and outcomes of creating new words by adding affixes to word roots, where a derivational affix forms a new word (Mgullu, 1999; Katamba & Stonham, 2019). Word formation in Swahili takes place through various methods.
These methods follow a specific procedure for generating forms with different characteristics. These methods include borrowing, abbreviation (acronyms, blending, and clipping), and extension. The compounding method uses complete words formed through various processes as raw material to create entirely new words. The resulting forms are written by joining two words together, using hyphens, or leaving a space between the combined words (Gichuru, 2010).
However, in this article we decided to write these compound nouns in two ways; that is, by combining all the words and by separating some of the words in these compound nouns in order to simplify the analysis of the data conducted. Mgullu (1999) views compound nouns as complex morphemes. He states that complex morphemes are morphemes formed by joining together at least two stems or roots of words, which, in other contexts, these stems can be separated and each one can be a complete word.
Mgullu’s views (already mentioned) are in agreement with those of Mustafa (2020), who maintains that compound nouns are nouns formed by joining two words (roots, stems, free morphemes, or lexical affixes). These two words may be joined with a space, without a space, or with a hyphen.

Kiparsky (1982) explained an approach to parenthesis deletion that emphasizes that the inner parentheses are deleted first at the lexical level.
As a result of this approach, words that overlap in the middle at the lexical level cannot be affected by cyclic phonological rules. After the parentheses have been deleted, words formed through morphology are treated as if they were newly created.
In Kiparsky’s (1982) view, this restriction extends to morphological processes, and the word formation rules cannot access the internal structure of words that originated at an earlier level. This knowledge was useful in the study that was conducted.
Gichuru (2010), in examining Swahili compound nouns, identified and classified the procedures and rules involved in the formation of compound nouns based on the six pillars of Lexical Morphology theory.
His study drew its data from Nyuso za Mwanamke (2010), Kamusi ya Isimu na Falsafa ya Lugha (2004), and Kamusi ya Kiswahili Sanifu (2004). The study focused on the forms of compound nouns, the rules and procedures involved in forming compound nouns, and the grammatical relationships between the morphemes of the compound nouns.
It is true that Gichuru's (2010) research had some similarities with the study we conducted, as both studies focused on the same type of data, namely compound nouns, and the theory of Lexical Morphology.
In his research on eliminating cyclicity as a source of phonological difficulty, Cole (1995) identified the internal morphology of the word as being accessible to phonology even after the word has been fully formed. He goes on to state that the internal morphological structure of the word must be visible in order to capture the effects of cyclicity by imposing constraints within domains that are morphologically defined. From this analysis, we gained insight into how internal morphology is reflected in phonology, a situation that contrasts with the Parenthesis Deletion Rule, which allows phonology to refer only to the external structure of the word. This helped us to recognize how some Swahili compound nouns violate the Parenthesis Deletion Rule by permitting phonology to access the internal structure of the compound noun after various morphemes have been joined.

Theory The Theory of Scientific Terminology was used to provide data for this article. The Theory of Scientific Terminology is associated with Kiingi (1989), who is the original founder of this theory.
Later, this theory was further developed by Kiingi (1992, 1998); Mwaro-Were (2000, 2001); Kevogo, Mukuthuria, and Ontieri (2018); and Kevogo (2020). This theory contains scientific criteria for effective terminology, which play a central role in the evaluation conducted in our article.
Several scholars, such as Tumbo-Masabo and Mwansoko (2008), have clearly outlined various principles that should be followed during the borrowing, adaptation, and formation of terminology from indigenous and foreign languages into Swahili. These principles are elaborated in Tumbo-Masabo and Mwansoko (2008).
This involves creating terminology using Swahili, Bantu languages, other African languages, and foreign languages. In addition to many other views expressed by Tumbo-Masabo and Mwansoko (1997) regarding these principles, they recommend the following guidelines: Terminology should reflect the distinctive features of the concepts they represent (or at least suggest such features) and be clear to the intended users. If terminology is formed using compounding and word combination techniques, the following aspects must be considered: The number of words combined or joined should not exceed two. Hyphens should not be used to separate compound terms, because in Swahili such terms are usually pronounced as a single word, with the primary stress on only one syllable. For example: -zao mtiririko ("flow products") and -nusu kipenyo ("semi-diameter"). Such terms should not have more than four syllables, since Swahili words usually have an average of between one and five syllables. Terminology must be highly derivable in order to generate other related terms within the relevant domain or field. This will allow terms representing closely related or derivationally connected concepts to logically reflect that connection. For example: taifa ("nation"), taifisha ("nationalize"), utaifa ("nationalism"), -a kitaifa ("national"), -sio na taifa ("stateless"), and -siyo wa taifa ("un-national").
Terminology (especially those with overlapping shades of meaning) must maintain consistency in sequence. The following terms, for example, demonstrate an acceptable alignment of sequence: tabakanje, tabakandani, tabakamzunguko, tabakakati, and so on. Terminology must be adequate, meaning it should reflect the characteristics of the concepts they represent (and not, for instance, contradict them). For example, the term “tahakiki ya maandishi” is inappropriate because it generally refers to the evaluation of writing at large, rather than the analysis or critique of literature. Terminology must also be short and understandable. Lengthy terms, such as “majira ya majani kupukutika” (when the dry season approaches), are unsuitable because they do not adhere to the principle of linguistic economy.
These principles are essential in terminology creation as they provide guidance on what should be done regarding borrowing, formation, and adaptation. In addition, methods for creating terminology have been proposed. Let us look at these methods below: first, translation, especially from languages known to be international, such as English, French, Spanish, and so on. Second, forming terminology based on conceptual frameworks. This theory is relevant as it provides the foundations to follow during the translation process.
