Keywords: DACIR Instructional Process; The development of Thai Literature; Teaching Activities Abstract Background and Objectives: The development of personnel as teachers in the Thai language major requires the enhancement of instructional management that promotes students’ achievement of the course objectives for the Development of Thai Literature. This relies on instructional activities that encourage students to formulate essential questions, set research hypotheses, and engage in learning processes aligned with the DACIR model, which aims to develop students’ critical thinking, analytical skills, and interaction with various information sources. Therefore, this research aims to: 1) develop learning activities for the course “Development of Thai Literature” using the DACIR instructional process; 2) compare learning outcomes in the course; and 3) study students’ satisfaction with learning activities in the course. Research Methodology: The research sample comprised 60 third-year undergraduate students majoring in Thai language at Thepsatri Rajabhat University, semester 1/2022, selected by simple random sampling. The research instruments were learning activities for the course based on the DACIR instructional process, an achievement test, and a satisfaction questionnaire. Data were analyzed using content validity, mean, standard deviation, and t-test. Research Results: 1) Learning activities in the “Development of Thai Literature” course using the DACIR instructional process showed the highest level of content validity overall. 2) Students' learning achievement in the course after instruction was significantly higher than before at the .05 level. 3) Overall, students were most satisfied with the implementation of learning activities in the course. Conclusion: Learning activities in the Thai literature course utilizing the DACIR method exhibit the highest content validity, result in high learning achievement, and are regarded as highly satisfactory by students overall. Introduction Literature is something that enriches intelligence and knowledge in readers in a diverse range of ways. For example, in terms of language, literature enables readers to understand word meanings, the use of language in different eras and regions, and the patterns of various poetic forms. In terms of history, literature serves as evidence that records chronicles, legends, folktales, and local stories. Furthermore, literature provides varied emotional values, as it is an art form that conveys emotions and feelings through expressive and lively language, allowing readers to share in those emotions and appreciate the aesthetic qualities of literature (Nannaphak Promma, 2020). This corresponds with the perspective of Ruennarit Satchapan (2006), who commented on the significance of reading literature, concluding that reading literature provides readers with broader and more diverse life experiences than their own, helps them understand human thoughts and actions in various situations, and offers insights into society, economy, culture, and beliefs. Additionally, literature reflects society, ways of life, livelihoods, traditions, culture, values, the existence of ancestors, customs, and various beliefs—elements that poets have interwoven into literary works. Since literature compiles social and cultural progress and elevates the mind, serving as an important historical record, the Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008) prescribes the study of literature and literary works, including analyzing literature for data, ideas, literary values, and enjoyment. It also involves learning about and understanding lullabies, children's play songs, and folk songs, which are valuable Thai heritage, conveying thoughts, values, customs, society of the past, and the beauty of language, so that students appreciate and take pride in the inherited wisdom of their ancestors. This is stipulated in Strand 5: Literature and Literary Works, with learning standards specifying that learners must understand, express opinions on, and critique Thai literature and literary works with appreciation and be able to apply them in real life (Ministry of Education, 2008). The Bachelor of Education program in Thai language (Revised Curriculum B.E. 2562/2019) recognizes the importance of studying the development of Thai literature by offering the course THL 2101208 Development of Thai Literature, focusing on students researching and analyzing the background and development of Thai literature in terms of form, concepts, content, literary techniques, comparison of literary characteristics, and the relationship between literature and the socio-political context of each era, as well as practicing analysis of significant Thai literary works from different periods as a guideline for teaching Thai literature in classrooms. Learners who complete this course will be able to use knowledge gained to improve their own teaching methods effectively as future Thai language teachers. The development of personnel as teachers in the Thai language major must therefore involve the development of instructional management aimed at enabling students to achieve the objectives of the Development of Thai Literature course. This requires the organization of teaching activities that focus on allowing students to pose key questions, formulate hypotheses in their studies, and interact with diverse historical sources or evidence of Thai literature, so that learners can construct their own knowledge in response to those key questions and hypotheses they have formulated.
Knowledge from other disciplines is utilized to aid in understanding or addressing the aforementioned issues, and findings are presented in tangible outcomes. This instructional approach is aligned with the DACIR Instructional Process, which aims to develop students' abilities to analyze, differentiate, and interact with a variety of information sources. It emphasizes students' engagement with various forms of evidence and the critical analysis and critique of those issues based on the evidence (Meredith, 2015; Moore, 2015). The DACIR Instructional Process developed here integrates historical methodology with a systematic approach to the study of historical evidence, following the sequence established by Drake and Brown (2003): steps 1, 2, and 3. Its objective is to enhance historical thinking skills as an instructional process that encourages students to seek historical facts through investigation, analysis, interpretation, and synthesis of historical narratives, and systematically trains students to analyze evidence. Students interpret and analyze primary historical evidence presented by instructors, critically engage with related historical data, and seek additional relevant evidence that may either support or contradict the instructor-specified issues in order to synthesize knowledge. Consideration of the relevant social and cultural contexts of each historical event is also included in order to foster broader historical perspectives and concepts. The process comprises five key steps as follows: 1) The discussion on the issue of the study (D): Deliberating on an event to define the essential questions, formulate hypotheses, and determine the content framework. 2) The analysis of the essential primary evidence (A): Preparing and examining one significant piece of primary evidence for discussion in various contexts. 3) The comparison of data (C): Increasing the amount of related primary and/or secondary evidence relevant to the issue under study, with no fewer than two pieces, to allow students to analyze, interpret, and link their findings for comparison with those from step 2. 4) Interaction with the historical evidence (I): Students themselves research and prepare additional primary and/or secondary evidence related to that in step 2, analyzing and interpreting it to form connections. 5) Report the finding (R): Students present their findings in a reasoned, coherent manner with factual references, including both supporting and opposing perspectives on the primary concept (Chairat Tosila, 2012; Narongrit Saksansri, 2017). This research therefore aims to study the application of the DACIR Instructional Process as a framework for developing learning activities in the development of Thai literature course for students. These activity-based learning approaches are intended to support student teachers—those about to graduate to become professional teachers—in obtaining the knowledge and understanding of the development of Thai literature and applying this knowledge as a guideline for future Thai language teaching and learning. Discussion of results 1.
The results of developing learning activities in the Development of Thai Literature course, using the DACIR Instructional Process for undergraduate students in the Bachelor of Education program, Thai language major, at Thepsatri Rajabhat University, revealed that the content validity, as assessed by experts, was at the highest level. This may be because the learning activities in the Development of Thai Literature course, employing the DACIR Instructional Process, were systematically designed by the researcher. The researcher studied the Bachelor of Education program, Thai language major, revised curriculum B.E. 2562, the course description for PH 2101208 Development of Thai Literature, relevant learning content from textbooks and documents, as well as the principles for creating learning activities and underlying concepts and theories. The data gathering concerning learning activity design using DACIR Instructional Process was based on various documents and websites, both domestic and international. The activities developed were sequenced according to the concept of the DACIR Instructional Process following historical methods, starting with Learning Unit 1: Determining Origins, designed to align with the first step—discussion to establish study issues on the origins and development of various forms of Thai literature, leading to the formulation of key questions, setting hypotheses, and defining the framework for studying the development of Thai literature. Learning Unit 2: Gathering and Organizing, was designed to correspond to the second step—analysis of primary evidence—where learners research and prepare a piece of primary evidence related to a major issue of study, under the instructor’s guidance. Citation: Chotchuang, C. (2023).
To enable students to discuss and analyze within various contexts, Learning Unit 3, "Debate and Synthesize," was designed to align with Step 3: Data Comparison. Students are required to research and prepare relevant primary and/or secondary evidence related to the issues under study, then analyze and interpret this evidence, linking the findings toward comparing them with those obtained in Step 2. Learning Unit 4, "Interpret and Integrate," was designed to correspond with Step 4, in which students research and prepare primary and/or secondary evidence that is related to evidence from Step 2, followed by analysis and interpretation to form interconnections. Learning Unit 5, "Present and Explain," involves students presenting the studied topic to address the hypothesis rationally, coherently, and with factual references—presenting both supporting and contradicting perspectives to the main concept. Once the activities were developed, they were submitted to experts for evaluation. The results and suggestions from the experts were then used for revision before pilot testing to identify activity flaws and evaluate their effectiveness. The outcomes and recommendations were used for further refinement before actual implementation. This is consistent with Phuwadon Wiriya (2018), who presented instructional activities on contemporary world events for upper secondary school students and found that the learning activities, structured as unit plans using the DACIR Instructional Process and consisting of four learning units, appropriately and feasibly addressed the nature of the course and the objectives of contemporary world studies. Experts assessed that students could perform the activities as intended, and the activities promoted students' achievement of learning objectives. This also aligns with Imchit Muadrae, Suntorn Onrit, and Chairat Tosila (2022), who studied the development of ASEAN Studies learning activities using activity-based learning to promote strong citizenship competencies among high school students. They found that the developed activities were sequenced along the historical timeline of Southeast Asia, comprising four learning units: 1) ASEAN Background, 2) ASEAN Founding, 3) ASEAN Today, and 4) ASEAN Future. The Index of Item Objectives Congruence (IOC) for all activities ranged from 0.80 to 1.00, with overall suitability rated at the highest level.
The results of comparing learning achievement in the Development of Thai Literature course using the DACIR Instructional Process for undergraduate students in the Bachelor of Education program, Thai language major, at Thepsatri Rajabhat University revealed that the students’ achievement after participating in the learning activities designed with the DACIR Instructional Process was significantly higher than before these activities at the .05 statistical level. This may be because, with the DACIR Instructional Process, students participated in learning activities through a systematic group process, enabling them to carry out tasks related to the study of issues concerning the development of Thai literature in class. Their outputs conformed to the specified assessment criteria, presenting content in a systematic manner consistent with the evidence found, with thorough analysis and interpretation of various documentary sources, as well as effective presentation. The study of students’ satisfaction regarding the organization of learning activities in the Development of Thai Literature course using the DACIR Instructional Process for undergraduate students in the Bachelor of Education program, Thai language major, at Thepsatri Rajabhat University, found that learners’ overall satisfaction with the learning activities in this course, when using the DACIR Instructional Process, was at the highest level. This may be because the learning activities in the Development of Thai Literature course emphasized full student participation. The historical approach of the DACIR Instructional Process was integrated with the study, enabling students to learn about the development of Thai literature step by step. Students were involved in researching evidence that led to important data in the study of Thai literature. As the instructor, the researcher reduced their own role to that of an advisor and facilitator, allowing all students to take the lead in organizing learning activities, thus encouraging everyone’s involvement. This resulted in engaging learning activities, with discussions on various topics that fostered interaction in the classroom and created an enthusiastic learning environment. The challenges presented promoted student interest in studying additional topics as well. These findings are consistent with those of Narongrit Saksansri (2017), who studied the effects of the DACIR Instructional Process on historical empathy and attitudes toward history learning among Grade 10 students, finding that students’ attitudes toward history learning after experiencing the DACIR process were significantly higher than before at the .05 level. Students had very high attitudes toward history learning in three aspects: 1) they liked learning history using historical methods because the activities challenged their abilities; 2) studying historical contexts made them feel more connected to people in the past; and 3) they enjoyed participating in classroom discussions about history. Keyword: Thai dessert, Khanom Thong Eak, Job’s Tears Flour, Sensory Evaluation Introduction Job’s Tears, also known by its common names Adlay, Adlay millet, or Job’s Tears (Coix lacryma-jobi L.), belongs to the grass family (POACEAE or GRAMINEAE) and the subfamily PANICOIDEAE. It is a type of cereal grain in the same family as rice. Job’s Tears is an annual plant with a monocotyledonous leaf. Its stem is soft-wooded, cylindrical, and upright, growing to a height of approximately 1-3 meters. It is native to Southeast Asia, and in Thailand, it is considered an economic crop commonly cultivated in the northern and northeastern regions. The plant has a distinctive aroma and is high in dietary fiber. The plant structure resembles that of corn, and its seeds are round to oval, white in color, with a slightly nutty taste. There are both edible and inedible varieties; the edible type has a softer fruit shell and is cultivated mainly for food purposes (Sunanta Thongtha, n.d.; Sunthorn Treenuntawan, 2010). Job’s Tears is a cereal grain with high nutritional value, rich in vitamins and minerals essential for the body. One hundred grams of Job’s Tears contains 372 kilocalories; 13.6 grams of protein, 3.7 grams of fat, 71.0 grams of carbohydrates, 3.4 grams of dietary fiber, 19 milligrams of calcium, 364 milligrams of phosphorus, 8.0 milligrams of iron, 0.38 milligrams of vitamin B1, 0.10 milligrams of vitamin B2, and 3.10 milligrams of vitamin B3 (Editorial Department, Sangdad Publishing, n.d.).
Job’s Tears also contains a variety of amino acids, such as glutamic acid, leucine, alanine, proline, valine, phenylalanine, isoleucine, arginine, among others. In addition, Job’s Tears has numerous health benefits and properties that help nourish and promote bodily health in various ways. For example, it helps improve the digestive system and reduces constipation, as Job’s Tears is very high in dietary fiber, thereby aiding smooth bowel movements. It also promotes restful sleep because it contains amino acids that stimulate the production of melatonin, a hormone that helps induce sleep. Furthermore, the vitamin A present in Job’s Tears supports eye health, enhancing the ability to see in low light or at night. It also strengthens teeth and bones, helps prevent osteoporosis, nourishes tendons and joints, and contributes to radiant skin. Job’s Tears is rich in vitamins B1 and B2; regular consumption can help make the skin more beautiful and vibrant (Vichaiyut Hospital, 2020; Anonymous, 2020). Khanom Thong Eak is a type of Thai dessert belonging to the “thong” (golden) family. It is presumed that this dessert did not originate in traditional Thai culture but instead was influenced by Indian or Middle Eastern Muslim cultures. The name “Thong Eak” carries an auspicious meaning, symbolizing wealth and prosperity. The ingredients of Khanom Thong Eak include flour mixed with egg yolk, sugar, and coconut milk. The preparation involves stirring the mixture until it forms a dough, which is then molded into beautiful shapes using intricately carved molds. After unmolding, the desserts are left to dry on the surface. Sometimes, they are scented with candle smoke for fragrance before being stored for eating. The finished dessert is fragrant, has a smooth and creamy sweet taste due to the sugar and coconut milk, and its color ranges from yellow to orange, varying from pale yellow or creamy yellow to deep orange, with small sheets of gold leaf attached for decoration, making it attractive. As such, Thong Eak is popularly given as a gift to bestow good fortune upon the recipient (Dinar Boontham & Waraporn Jiwchaisak, 2014). However, it can be observed that the main ingredients used in Khanom Thong Eak are wheat flour, coconut milk, granulated sugar, and egg yolks from chicken or duck eggs. Consuming these in large amounts regularly can result in excessive intake of sugar, fat, and cholesterol—beyond the body's requirements—which may lead to various diseases (Sirimon Chaikate, Pornpen Morakotjinda, and Weena Thongrod, 2017), such as NCDs (Noncommunicable Diseases). NCDs are chronic non-communicable diseases that do not result from pathogens and cannot be transmitted from person to person but are caused by long-term unhealthy habits and behaviors, gradually leading to disease without being immediately apparent. NCDs include diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, obesity, central obesity, heart diseases, and vascular diseases, among others. Therefore, this research sought to utilize flour made from Job’s Tears as an ingredient in the development of Thai dessert products, given its nutritional benefits and health value. Additionally, Job’s Tears is a locally cultivated plant in the northern and northeastern regions of Thailand, which helps promote the use of agricultural products among farmers. The aim is to create a Thai dessert that is nutritious and rich in various nutrients suitable for all genders and ages. Thus, Job’s Tears flour was developed as a key component in Thong Eak to supplement the standard recipe, studying the basic formula and appropriate quantity of Job’s Tears flour, in order to obtain a well-accepted Thong Eak product. 40 No.1 January-March 2025 The Effectiveness of Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy Programs for Depression: A Systematic Literature Review Background and Significance of the Problem Depression is a major global issue affecting societies and economies worldwide.1 More than 50% of individuals who experience depression for the first time will experience a recurrence, and the risk of recurrence increases over time.2 Studies in Thailand have found that the recurrence rate for depression is 38.63%. Recurrence and the onset of new episodes of depression depend on the follow-up period—the longer the monitoring, the higher the chances of observing recurrence or new episodes. The primary treatment for depression is antidepressant medication, which can have side effects, and some patients may not be able to tolerate these side effects for long.4 Therefore, psychosocial treatments without medication or a combination of medication and psychotherapy are important and essential for patients with depression. In 2002, Segal, Williams, and Teasdale5 developed the Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) program, a psychological treatment combining mindfulness practice and cognitive development, integrating Kabat-Zinn’s Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR)6 and Beck’s Cognitive Therapy7 models for treating patients with depression. Patients with depression often lack awareness of distorted thinking patterns, which become habitual and lead to cognitive abnormalities, such as negative bias, rumination on past events, resulting in sad emotions and unsuitable behaviors. Therefore, the therapy integrates mindfulness programs to reduce stress and adjust thought and behavioral patterns for depression treatment, a method known as MBCT. MBCT has also been adapted to address stress and anxiety in the general population and to promote well-being.5,8 Later, MBCT was used to treat patients with recurrent depression and to reduce depressive symptoms in those with physical illnesses.9 The MBCT program is typically group-based, with eight continuous sessions over eight weeks, occurring once per week. During the week, patients are assigned mindfulness activities to practice at home and encouraged to share their experiences within the group, allowing members to learn from each other's experiences and reinforce resilience using cognitive therapy techniques such as self-talk, guided imagery, role-playing, cognitive reframing, and schema modification. Mindfulness practice begins with basic meditation training, both in movement and stillness, encouraging members to cultivate careful awareness and self-reflection.10 MBCT is a therapeutic concept combining mindfulness practice and cognitive therapy to help patients learn, grow, and relieve symptoms through ongoing practice and awareness of thoughts, feelings, and emotions, leading to self-acceptance in the present moment without clinging to personal judgment. This approach fosters awareness of thoughts and feelings as they arise, changes responses to stimuli based on present realities, and enables separation from negative thoughts.10 MBCT emphasizes the patient’s perception of the connection between thoughts and feelings, increasing meta-cognitive awareness. Negative thoughts and reactive behaviors often stem from automatic, unconscious responses. Through MBCT, patients gradually learn to shift from mindless to mindful patterns of behavior,5 a transition from “doing mode” (automatic, habit-based responses that perpetuate obstacles and negative thinking) to “being mode” (mindful, present-moment awareness, characterized by acceptance and nonjudgmental openness).8 People often form ideas about how things should be, compare these expectations to current realities, and unconsciously create actions to close the gap. This cycle can persist without awareness, resulting in ongoing dissatisfaction and frustration.5 In contrast, mindfulness embodies “acceptance” and “permission” to let things be, maintaining presence and openness.8 Mindless and mindful actions differ in their timing of attention. Mindfulness brings consideration of the consequences and enables proactive reflection, keeping the mind in the present. Mindless conduct often dwells in the future or past, causing the mind to leave the present moment. Maintaining mindfulness requires serious practice; with enhanced mindfulness, attentiveness to the present improves. Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy for Depression aims to reduce depressive symptoms and prevent recurrence as part of a non-pharmacological treatment, with more than 20 years of development and effectiveness testing. The program has been found to reduce depressive symptoms and prevent relapse,3 as well as increase mindfulness and decrease rumination.8 Most international research and knowledge about MBCT have focused on relapse prevention and symptoms reduction. In Thailand, its application usually integrates Buddhist concepts with counseling to support those with mental health challenges in various organizations.11 However, Beck’s Cognitive Therapy7 and MBCT as per Segal et al.5,8, which integrate mindfulness techniques for preventing depressive relapse, are still rarely used among depression patients in Thailand. Therefore, a systematic review of the effectiveness of MBCT programs for depression is necessary to guide adaptation of MBCT to the Thai context and expand its application for those with depression. Effectiveness of MBCT in Preventing Recurrence or New Onset of Depression Effectiveness of MBCT in preventing recurrence or onset of new episodes among patients with at least 3 previous episodes of depression, compared to treatment as usual, showed reduction in both recurrence and new onsets, and lowered the need for antidepressants.15 Comparing MBCT to long-term antidepressant use among patients with three or more depressive episodes and over two years of follow-up, there were no differences in recurrence/new episode timing or severe side effects. Other applications include patients with gastrointestinal inflammation26 and online MBCT for breast and prostate cancer patients27. 1 Book Review / Book Critique John Bresnan.
2006.
Jakarta: Equinox Publishing.
213 p. ISBN 979-3780-34-7.
Indonesia is one of the major economic and political powers in Southeast Asia, both regionally and globally, to the extent that it can be considered a middle power. Its large population, sizable economy, vast territory, and abundant natural resources have greatly contributed to Indonesia’s voice and role in the Southeast Asian region. In addition, historical circumstances such as Indonesia’s past as a colony of European great powers, its battle against Japanese forces during the Second World War, and the political turmoil it faced, particularly regarding communism during the Cold War, led Indonesia to develop close diplomatic relations with other major powers like the United States. However, this relationship has experienced both ups and downs. The book At Home Abroad: A Memoir of the Ford Foundation in Indonesia, 1953-1973, is authored by John Bresnan. The author served as Assistant Representative of the Ford Foundation in Indonesia from 1961 to 1965 and as Representative from 1969 to 1973. He also held the position of Head of the Asia and Pacific Office at the Ford Foundation headquarters in New York from 1973 to 1982. Bresnan wrote this book to share his experiences working with the Ford Foundation and his work in Indonesia. His close collaboration with Indonesia spanned many years and earned him the Distinguished Service Star (Bintang Jasa Pratama) from President Megawati Sukarnoputri in 2001.
In 2003, as an American serving as a liaison between the United States and Indonesia (Bresnan 2006, 1), this book recounts Bresnan’s personal experiences, attempting to answer key questions, including: Why did the Ford Foundation provide assistance in Indonesia for many years? Why did the Foundation keep staff in Indonesia and establish an organizational structure different from other American foundations? What did Ford Foundation representatives do in Indonesia? Was it worthwhile? And, if given the opportunity, would the author take up that role again? (Bresnan 2006, 2) The book is organized into 30 chapters, each relatively short, making it easy for readers to follow. In chapters 1-4, the author shares personal stories about the beginning of his work with the Ford Foundation. By chapter 5, the narrative shifts to the close relationship between the Ford Foundation and Indonesia, exploring why the Foundation was interested in providing assistance there. Bresnan explains this through a study of a Ford Foundation memorandum entitled “Memorandum on Foundation Group Going to Indonesia and Burma.” This document discussed various points, such as Indonesia’s strategic location, as well as its population of over 75 million at the time—making it the sixth largest country in the world then—and its significant natural resources, such as oil, rubber, and tin (Bresnan 2006, 27). The memorandum further stated that the most crucial point was that, despite Indonesia’s many internal political problems during that period, it had the potential to become a close ally of the democratic world. Although Indonesia had previously been a close ally of the Soviet Union and had domestic issues with communism, it had not transformed into a communist country (Bresnan 2006, 27). However, a critical problem facing Indonesia’s development was the lack of human resources developed through education or training—whether in public administration, teaching, economics, or medicine. At that time, Indonesia also lacked a national lingua franca, which impeded internal communication. Furthermore, Indonesians did not possess English-language proficiency comparable to Indians who had been under British rule (Bresnan 2006, 28). Thus, in order to support the United States’ important leadership role in global politics during that period, the U.S. should use the Ford Foundation as a vehicle for generating and disseminating knowledge and understanding of various countries or regions (Bresnan 2006, 28). Accordingly, in 1951, the Ford Foundation granted funding to Stanford University to survey the status of Asian Studies knowledge in the United States. The following year, in 1952, the Foundation provided fellowships for scholars to study Asia and the Middle East, and in the same year, over $125,000 was allocated to the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) to conduct research on development economics and political stability in India and Indonesia (Bresnan 2006, 28-29). Meanwhile, Ali Sastroamidjojo, then Indonesian ambassador in Washington D.C., expressed to the Institute for International Education (IIE) in New York his desire for assistance in sending English teachers to Indonesia to help its people learn English—serving both as a tool for economic development and “as an instrument for building democracy in a country very much in need of friends” (Bresnan 2006, 31). Additionally, Sumitro Djojohadikusumo, Minister of Finance and Dean of the Faculty of Economics at the University of Indonesia, hoped the United States would dispatch economists to teach at the university for a few years, sharing knowledge with Indonesians until they could develop their own cadre of economists (Bresnan 2006, 31-32). If this book is read from an international relations perspective, particularly as the author describes his time as a Ford Foundation officer during the Cold War, it offers various stories set against the backdrop of international politics in the Cold War era that affected the political and economic relations of the United States, with Indonesia as the focal point. The book also discusses the development of academic relations between Indonesia and the United States, illustrating that not only diplomatic channels were key instruments in international relations, but that academic routes were also employed to generate knowledge, foster understanding of differences, and nurture bilateral relations. Moreover, the Indonesian domestic political context is vividly portrayed in this account, allowing the reader to see how internal politics impacted Indonesia-U.S. relations—for example, Indonesians' views on U.S. financial assistance, and U.S. concerns that such aid might be diverted by the Indonesian government for military uses against Malaysia (Bresnan 2006, 48-51). However, Indonesia's own political turmoil and its conflict with Malaysia ultimately led to the closure of the Foundation in 1965. Bresnan explains in Chapter 11 that, during its presence in Indonesia from 1953 to 1965, the Ford Foundation allocated over $15 million to Indonesia, supported more than 400 Indonesian students to study abroad (mainly at the master’s and doctoral levels) to prepare them for university teaching careers—emphasizing English language, environmental sciences, mathematics, and economics (Bresnan 2006, 66). The Ford Foundation also played a significant role in helping the U.S. develop a body of scholarship on Indonesian studies. The Foundation supported academic research, explicitly not “intelligence work disguised as scholarship.” Thus, it established the International Training and Research (ITR) unit in New York. ITR funded MIT and Cornell University to conduct research on Indonesia, with early MIT research projects led by Max Millikan, head of the Center for International Studies, focusing on the political economy of Indonesia. Project topics included Indonesian financial structure (Douglas Paauw as researcher), national income estimation (Benjamin Higgins and Williams Hollinger), and environmental politics (Guy Pauker) (Bresnan 2006, 76). Other research initiatives included a policy-oriented project on village life in Java, led by researchers from Harvard University. The first book published from this project was Clifford Geertz’s “The Religion of Java” (Bresnan 2006, 77). Another project, on Indonesian politics, was led by George Kahin at Cornell University, who sought not only to study the role of communism in Indonesia but to address much broader issues, a view supported by the Foundation (Bresnan 2006, 79). Kahin would later recruit his student, Benedict Anderson, to conduct research on Indonesian issues. The Ford Foundation closed its operations in 1965.
In 1965, because the United States was dissatisfied with Indonesia over the incident in which the Indonesian government sent people to protest and burn down the British Embassy in Jakarta, Indonesia—an event that stemmed from conflicts between the Indonesian and British governments regarding Borneo and the establishment of Malaysia as a country—the burning of the embassy led the United States government to decide to withdraw various forms of assistance to Indonesia (Bresnan 2006, 47-48). The Foundation was relaunched again in the year...
In 1969, Adam Malik, then Minister of Foreign Affairs, requested that the foundation consider reopening (Bresnan 2006, 81-82). With this new beginning, the foundation had broader opportunities for engagement, as there were now five economists from the University of Indonesia who had studied in the United States funded by the foundation and had returned to occupy significant positions in various agencies, including: (1) Widjojo Nitisastro, who was the first Indonesian economist to graduate from the University of California, Berkeley, and who became head of the National Planning Agency; (2) Ali Wardhana, who received his Ph.D. from Berkeley and became Minister of Finance; (3) Emil Salim, who earned his doctorate from Berkeley and returned as Deputy Head of the Planning Agency; (4) Sadli, who received his master's degree from MIT and continued further studies at Berkeley and Harvard, who served as head of the Team on Capital Investment; and (5) Subroto, who earned his master's degrees from McGill University and MIT, and returned as head of planning at the Department of Trade. These five individuals became known as “The Berkeley Mafia” (Bresnan 2006, 93). The author explains in Chapter 181 the roles of all five in the country's economic development planning, highlighting the relationship between the formation of technocrats and national development. From Chapter 18 to Chapter 24, the author recounts experiences of the Foundation’s work in developing various issues in Indonesia such as the development of universities in the provinces, planning for rice development, rural development, education, and the advancement of social science research in Indonesia, which received assistance from Clifford Geertz. In summary, although this book is not written as an academic or theoretical debate—for it recounts experiences working with the Ford Foundation during the Cold War, as well as experiences related to economic, social, and especially university-level education development (which is a major theme of the book)—it presents these events against the backdrop of the Cold War, the conflict between Indonesia and Malaysia, and internal political issues. One important dimension from reading this book is that it allows us to see the Ford Foundation as an actor that serves as donor, researcher, or agency supporting research and activities. If one shifts the perspective from the activities themselves and focuses on the foundation as an actor, one finds that understanding the role of non-state actors in the international relations system—in supporting education, research, or development aid during the Cold War—was critically important to international relations. Reading to understand the roles and responsibilities of agencies supported by the government—or to be more precise, the Ford Foundation as part of the United States' foreign policy instruments—helps illuminate one of the networks of actors operating in tandem with policy and strategy during the Cold War. A perspective that may be drawn from this book is the attempt to explain the role of granting scholarships for scholars to study abroad and, upon their return, how they transfer political and economic ideas back to their own countries, transferring knowledge from Western countries to countries with different systems of government. The transformation of knowledge within universities, as institutions responsible for imparting knowledge and shaping social actors, means that this book serves as a case study for those interested in theories of learning and knowledge transfer from the democratic world to others, as well as the roles of technocrats educated overseas and their influence on their home countries' development. Another important quality that adds to this book’s value for study is the documentation referenced by the author. As previously mentioned, although this book does not conform to academic conventions—such as providing a full bibliography at the end—the extensive footnotes lend credibility to the sources, making the book not merely a recounting of facts or personal views, but one that incorporates primary documents: meeting minutes, letters, or other official records. As a result, the content is rich with topics and information of interest and will be very useful for those wishing to pursue related research further. Keywords: Cocoa beans, Drying performance, Heat pump dryer From field observations, one significant issue in the cocoa product processing steps is the moisture reduction stage of cocoa beans following fermentation. Currently, occupational groups sun-dry the beans using solar heat, making it impossible to control drying temperatures and ambient relative humidity, which in turn reduces product quality. With expertise in drying technology, the research team proposes a solution using heat pump dryer technology. The first step involves studying the effects of controlled parameters on the drying performance of cocoa beans with a laboratory-scale heat pump dryer, serving as a guideline for designing heat pump dryers for such occupational groups. Cocoa is an alternative crop that can be grown as a substitute or as an intercrop in mixed plantations. It is easy to maintain, fast-growing, drought-resistant, and produces high yields. Once processed, it has a fragrant aroma and is in market demand. After harvesting, the fresh cocoa beans are not yet chocolate. The beans are transformed into chocolate during the fermentation process. The method farmers use to ferment fresh cocoa beans depends on market requirements and consumer demand. Laongsri (2020) suggested in the cocoa knowledge manual that fermenting fresh cocoa beans takes 6 days, with the cocoa piles being turned on the 2nd and 4th days of fermentation to allow microorganisms to function thoroughly throughout the process. The beans are then sun-dried until their moisture content reaches 5–7%. Once fully dried, they can be stored for many years awaiting sale. The drying duration critically affects the taste and quality of the dried cocoa beans. If drying is too slow, mold may penetrate the beans, altering the cocoa flavor. Sun drying is suitable for countries harvesting cocoa in the dry season. Cocoa beans may be dried on dirt yards or cement yards lined with plastic, or on wooden racks or elevated thick plastic mesh trays covered with a plastic roof. This method takes about 7 days to dry. Hii et al.
(2006) conducted research on drying different quantities of cocoa beans—20, 30, and 60 kilograms—using a solar energy dryer and found that the drying times were 5, 7.5, and 9.5 days, respectively. For dryers applied at the community enterprise level, most cases reduce energy costs by using biomass dryers (Chunkaew et al., 2024; Khadwilard et al., 2024). For large-scale farmers who need to harvest cocoa during the rainy season, when rainfall is frequent and sun-drying is challenging, ovens are used to dry the cocoa beans. The most commonly used ovens are of two types: (1) Non-fan ovens, such as the Samoa oven, where the lower part contains iron pipes for fuel and a smoke vent at the back. This type of oven uses conduction heating and takes approximately two days to dry at temperatures of 60–80 °C. The drying time may vary according to the thickness of the cocoa beans in the trays. (2) Fan-assisted ovens are more efficient than the Samoa type because the fan distributes hot air uniformly across the trays (Horticultural Research Institute, 2021). Heat pump dryer technology has the advantage of high performance and can dry cocoa beans under low humidity conditions using a closed-air system (Achariyaviriya et al., 2005), as it uses an evaporator to extract moisture from the air and recovers heat back into the heat pump system. Its limitation is that it cannot achieve high drying air temperatures. According to published articles, when using only a heat pump system without auxiliary heating coils, the maximum drying temperature is 60 °C in a closed circulation system (Chunkaew, 2021; Guan et al., 2025). For comparison, when drying kaffir lime leaves at the same temperature, resistance coil dryers consumed more specific energy than heat pump dryers (Sanpang et al., 2022). In addition, published work on drying cocoa beans using a heat pump dryer combined with heating coils at 56 °C reported a drying time of 26 hours (Hii et al., 2013). Therefore, the objective of this article is to study the effects of controlled parameters on the drying performance of cocoa beans using a heat pump dryer, as a guideline for developing heat pump dryers for cocoa product processing groups in Tak Province.
Research Methods 2.1 Heat Pump Dryer Figure 1 shows a small heat pump dryer with walls insulated with fiberglass, developed from Chunkaew (2021). The electric valve in the heat pump system, which was previously installed before the expansion valve, was removed to address the issue of the electric valve malfunctioning, which caused high-side pressure buildup and increased compressor workload. Additionally, the blower was replaced to allow for reduced speed by adjusting the frequency via an inverter. The heat pump system uses a hermetically sealed compressor, Kultorn AE1360Y model, with a capacity of 497 W. The condenser can transfer heat at 1,800 W, and the evaporator can transfer heat at 1,300 W, both of which are automotive components. The operating principle of the heat pump dryer is as follows: When the heat pump system is turned on, the condenser transfers heat to the air at the set temperature. The heat in the air is transferred to the drying material, causing moisture in the cocoa beans to evaporate. Consequently, the air temperature exiting the drying chamber decreases, but the moisture content in the air increases. The air leaving the drying chamber passes through the evaporator (40% of the air), while 60% of the air is bypassed past the evaporator. The portion passing through the evaporator experiences a drop in temperature, causing the water vapor to be condensed on the cold pipes of the evaporator and then discharged outside the dryer. The reason for bypassing some air past the evaporator is due to the large amount of heat in the air leaving the chamber; without bypassing, the water vapor in the hot air would not condense into liquid at the evaporator. Finally, as the moisture on the surface of the drying material decreases, the moisture inside the material migrates to the surface. For temperature control, a TOHO TTM-004 controller is used to control the operation of the compressor motor, stopping its operation when the set temperature is reached. Experimental Results and Discussion 3.1 Effects of Temperature and Air Velocity on the Change in Drying Rate The drying of cocoa beans using a small-scale heat pump dryer was conducted at temperatures of 40, 50, and 60 °C with air velocities of 1.5, 3.5, and 5.5 m/s, and an air flow ratio across the evaporator of 60%. Drying was carried out from the initial to the final moisture content. It was found that at 40 °C with air velocities of 1.5, 3.5, and 5.5 m/s, the drying time required was 660 minutes (11 hours). At 50 °C and an air velocity of 1.5 m/s, the drying time was 540 minutes (9 hours), while at air velocities of 3.5 and 5.5 m/s, the drying time was 480 minutes (8 hours). Lastly, at 60 °C and air velocities of 1.5 and 3.5 m/s, the drying time was 480 minutes (8 hours), whereas at an air velocity of 5.5 m/s, the drying time was 420 minutes (7 hours). In comparison with the study by Chungsiriporn et al. (2023), which used a hot air dryer with an electric coil at 60 °C and an air velocity of 0.1 m/s, a drying time of 485 minutes was reported. Therefore, the heat pump dryer achieved shorter drying times, likely due to higher air velocities and lower relative humidity in the hot air. Furthermore, the removal of moisture by the evaporator resulted in a higher heat transfer rate than the hot air dryer with an electric coil, although the general trend remained similar. As shown in Figure 5, at the beginning of the drying process, the drying rate was high and gradually decreased over time in all conditions. This is because at first, water is present on the surface of the cocoa beans, and as the weight decreases, internal moisture slowly migrates to the surface, resulting in a lower drying rate towards the end. Thus, increases in temperature and air velocity lead to higher drying rates. This is because, with elevated temperatures and velocities, the heat content of pre-drying air rises, enabling more moisture inside the cocoa beans to change from liquid to vapor phases, as illustrated in Figure 6. These findings are consistent with other drying technologies; for example, higher drying temperatures generally shorten drying times, such as in the drying of whole bananas using a biomass dryer (Chunkaew et al., 2018) and cocoa beans with a hot air electric coil dryer (Herman et al., 2018). Correspondently, banana drying using a heat pump dryer with R32 refrigerant at 50, 55, and 60 °C and air velocities of 1, 1.5, and 2 m/s (Somsila et al., 2023), as well as radish slices dried using a heat pump dryer combined with a recovery wheel at 35, 40, and 45 °C and an air velocity of 1 m/s (Yan et al., 2025), demonstrated similar results. 3.2 Effects of Temperature and Air Velocity on Specific Moisture Extraction Rate (SMER) Figure 7 shows that as temperature and air velocity increase, the specific moisture extraction rate (SMER) decreases. This occurs because higher temperatures and air velocities reduce the drying time, but the compressor motor power increases in correspondence with velocity and temperature, and the blower motor power increases as higher frequencies are required for blower operation. This finding corresponds with the drying of banana slices at 60 °C with three airflow rates—0.117, 0.128, and 0.140 m³/s—using a heat pump dryer (Chunkaew, 2021). However, it differs from the banana drying by a heat pump dryer using R32 refrigerant at 50, 55, and 60 °C, with air velocities of 1, 1.5, and 2 m/s (Somsila et al., 2023), because the temperature control systems were different: this study employed a compressor motor running at all times with temperature regulation via bypassing refrigerant to the external condenser at set points, while the compared system controlled by stopping the compressor motor. 3.3 Effects of Temperature and Air Velocity on the Coefficient of Performance of the Heat Pump (COPh) The behavior of the heat pump’s coefficient of performance (COPh) when adjusting temperature and air velocity was examined. Initially, during the early stages of drying at all velocities, the COPh increases as the dryer is started and temperature rises to the target. At this stage, the system is not yet in a steady-state, as part of the supplied heat is used for heating the structure and the rest for raising the temperature of the cocoa beans. Once the dryer system reaches equilibrium and the moisture content of the cocoa beans decreases, the COPh tends to decline (Figure 8). Figure 9 presents the average COPh test results: at all air velocities, the highest COPh was recorded at 60 °C, followed by 50 °C, and the lowest at 40 °C. This is because increasing the drying air temperature raises the pressure and temperature of the refrigerant on the discharge side of the compressor, resulting in an increase in compressor work (Wcomp) and heat flow at the condenser (Q̇ac). Additionally, higher air velocity was found to increase the COPh, in agreement with the findings for drying banana slices at 60 °C with three airflow rates (0.117, 0.128, and 0.140 m³/s) using a heat pump dryer (Chunkaew, 2021). 3.4 Summary of Drying Performance of Cocoa Beans Using a Heat Pump Dryer At 40 °C and air velocities of 1.5, 3.5, and 5.5 m/s, the average drying rates (DR) were 0.78±0.008, 0.79±0.007, and 0.85±0.002 g/min, respectively; the average SMER were 358.77±20.98, 302.55±1.02, and 261.72±14.23 g/kWh, respectively; and the COPh were 0.85±0.23, 1.56±0.39, and 2.01±0.49, respectively. At 50 °C and air velocities of 1.5, 3.5, and 5.5 m/s, the average DR were 1.11±0.005, 1.25±0.007, and 1.25±0.002 g/min, respectively; the average SMER were 336.57±13.53, 296.65±15.34, and 229.65±10.39 g/kWh, respectively; and the COPh were 2.45±0.74, 2.93±0.74, and 3.36±0.86, respectively. At 60 °C and air velocities of 1.5, 3.5, and 5.5 m/s, the average DR were 1.20±0.002, 1.25±0.007, and 1.37±0.004 g/min, respectively; the average SMER were 240.74±11.30, 236.21±14.40, and 223.36±7.78 g/kWh, respectively; and the COPh were 3.94±0.08, 4.48±0.20, and 5.17±0.10, respectively. 3.5 Effects of Temperature and Air Velocity on the Color Quality of Cocoa Beans According to Table 1, as temperature and air velocity increased, the lightness (L*) and yellowness (b*) values decreased, while the redness (a*) value increased. This is because higher temperature and velocity result in greater heat applied during drying, which enhances browning reactions. These results align with studies on agricultural product drying, such as the drying of longan using high-temperature hot air (Chunthaworn et al., 2012; Khadwilard et al., 2025) and the color changes in okra dried by microwave, where increases in microwave power led to decreased lightness (L*) and yellowness (b*) but increased redness (a*) (Dadali et al., 2007). The development of EF (Executive Function) skills refers to a set of mental processes that help us think rationally, exercise self-restraint, regulate our emotions and behaviors, plan, work effectively, stay focused, avoid distraction, remember instructions, and efficiently handle multiple tasks to completion. This includes sequencing tasks step-by-step, adhering to goals, and following through until successful completion. EF skills are vitally important for humans in terms of academic and work success, careers, and building relationships with others (Office of the Narcotics Control Board, 2015). The functioning of the frontal lobes, particularly the areas involved in command and regulation, are related to one’s self, perception, observation, memory, analysis, reasoning, and problem-solving—encompassing a diverse range of higher-order thinking abilities. These functions regulate, control, and manage oneself, play an important role in setting goals, planning, organizing, and prioritizing tasks, which are expressed physically and mentally (Sakchai Jaisuetong, 2018). Preda Ulita (2016, as cited in Priyanan Phromsukkun, 2018) further asserts that “executive function skills for successful living” are the mental processes that enable individuals to organize, focus, follow instructions, and complete multiple tasks successfully. The brain relies on these abilities to filter information, sequence tasks, remember essential information for achieving tasks according to the set plan and goals, maintain self-control, persistence, and unwavering attention during activities. These elements provide crucial reinforcement and support for children, enabling them to develop and learn appropriately. While children are not naturally born with these skills, they are born with the potential to grow and develop them through interaction and practice. Most documents refer to EF (Executive Function) skills as “brain skills for successful life management,” comprising three components and nine specific skills: 1) working memory, 2) cognitive flexibility, 3) inhibitory control, 4) planning and organization, 5) goal-directed persistence, 6) initiative taking, 7) attention control, 8) emotional regulation, and 9) self-monitoring and evaluation. These nine skills form a new body of knowledge that early childhood caregivers, parents, guardians, or teachers should pay attention to, as EF skills are fundamental brain abilities. Meaning and significance of creative media derived from local wisdom materials [307] Citation: Puttasarin, T. (2024).
Educational scholars define local wisdom as objects found within a community or items invented and crafted from leftover or locally sourced materials. This also includes behaviors, games, activities, or patterns of action that reflect the customs, traditions, beliefs, and values of individuals in the community—elements that have been passed down from ancestors and continuously upheld by community members, becoming unique characteristics of each locality. For early childhood, local wisdom and media crafted from indigenous materials are significant for the following reasons (Office of the National Education Commission, 2010): 2.1 They enable children to learn through their five senses and gain firsthand experiences from local learning resources. With learning sources close at hand, children can seek out knowledge independently, practice directly, and develop positive knowledge, skills, and attitudes. They also build a sense of pride in their accomplishments. 2.2 They provide children with toys and play equipment. At this age, children learn through play, and access to such materials fosters creative play that becomes more complex and prolonged. The Meaning and Importance of Creative Media from Local Wisdom Materials Supawadee Hanmethee (2015) stated that the components of executive function (EF) skills for a successful life are as follows. Foundational skills group: 1) Working memory refers to the brain process used to remember, organize, and retrieve information. Information gained from various experiences is stored in the brain; working memory is the process that activates, moves, and selects appropriate information for use. This enables individuals to recall multiple pieces of information simultaneously and use them to successfully carry out various tasks. Working memory plays a critical role in life, from mental arithmetic and reading comprehension to following rules and regulations. 2) Inhibitory control is the ability to deliberate and reflect, allowing a person to regulate and filter thoughts and impulses, to resist temptations, agitation, or habitual responses, and to pause and think before acting. This allows individuals to select, focus, maintain attention, prioritize, and control their actions. Such abilities help prevent someone from behaving solely on instinct or doing whatever they feel like without thinking. It is the skill that helps a person concentrate on what matters rather than being aimless, enables them to say what is appropriate, and, when angry or frustrated, to control themselves and not shout or hit others; when feeling troubled, they can pause and thus complete their tasks. 3) Shift or cognitive flexibility refers to the skill that allows one to “shift gears” and adapt appropriately to changing environmental demands, whether related to changes in time, priorities, or goals. It allows an individual to flexibly apply different rules in different situations, learn to let go, notice mistakes and correct them, and adjust their methods based on new information. It also helps them to consider issues from their own perspective and think outside the box. Self-regulation skills group: 4) Attention refers to the ability to maintain alertness and direct attention toward the tasks necessary to achieve desired goals with focus and sustained awareness, for an age-appropriate duration and level of task difficulty. Attention is another fundamental quality essential for learning and performance. Some children may be highly intelligent but lack attention; when faced with internal or external stimuli, they lose concentration and are unable to continue working, resulting in incomplete tasks. 5) Emotional control refers to the ability to manage one’s emotions, to be aware of one’s emotional state, to adjust emotions suitably to match the situation, and to appropriately control emotional and behavioral expressions. Children who cannot manage their emotions may become irritable, easily angered, overreact to minor issues, and may be prone to anxiety, mood swings, or depression. 6) Self-monitoring is the skill of checking one’s feelings, thoughts, or actions, both during and after task completion, to ensure they lead to the desired goal. If any shortcomings or errors occur, these can be promptly corrected. This skill also promotes self-awareness regarding one’s needs.
Strengths and weaknesses become more evident, as does the examination of one’s own thoughts, feelings, or sense of self, monitoring and following up on one’s own reactions, and observing the outcomes of one’s behavior and its effects on others. Performance skill group: 7) Initiating – the ability to generate ideas, reflect, make decisions about what needs to be done, and then take action to implement those thoughts into tangible results. Individuals who demonstrate initiative must possess courage, decisiveness, and a willingness to take risks and learn from mistakes. This skill is fundamental to creative thinking and leads to the development of new innovations. 8) Planning and Organizing – the process that begins with planning, connecting essential components, such as setting goals, seeing the overall picture of the tasks, and determining activities. This involves transforming anticipated future events into tangible outcomes, setting goals, and laying out the steps in advance. It requires imagining or predicting situations beforehand and devising guidelines to achieve success according to set objectives. The process then proceeds to implementation, managing tasks to completion, breaking down goals into steps, organizing systems and mechanisms, and carrying out operations from beginning to end. This also includes managing space, materials, and time both efficiently and flexibly to ensure project success. 9) Goal-Directed Persistence – the ability to plan for goal attainment and constantly keep this information in mind throughout the task until completion, including being mindful of time and the ability to self-motivate and track ongoing progress toward the goal. Results of the Review of Pulmonary Tuberculosis Patient Care in Nonthaburi Province A study of treatment outcomes for pulmonary tuberculosis patients in Nonthaburi Province in 2021 found that 1,563 patients were registered for treatment at hospitals in the area. The hospitals with the highest number of tuberculosis cases were the Chest Disease Institute and Phra Nang Klao Hospital. Among these patients, the treatment success rate was 74.53 percent, the rate of treatment interruption was 5.11 percent, the treatment failure rate was 1.72 percent, the transfer-out rate was 1.15 percent, the mortality rate was 6.07 percent, and patients still undergoing treatment accounted for 11.42 percent (Tuberculosis Division, Department of Disease Control, 2022). From brainstorming sessions between researchers and the study sample, it was found that public health officers in the area still lacked understanding about tuberculosis, appropriate counseling on patient care during treatment, and correct measures for preventing the spread of tuberculosis. In addition, the majority of directly observed therapy (DOT) supervisors were patients’ relatives (65.48 percent), who generally lacked knowledge about proper medication administration. Local health officers also lacked coordination and consistent follow-up with patients, resulting in interrupted medication adherence. Furthermore, officers had insufficient knowledge and experience in tuberculosis patient care and ineffective infection control measures. There was also a lack of knowledge regarding tuberculosis prevention, control guidelines, and community-based care. Additionally, staff at tuberculosis clinics were ineffective in communicating and referring patients into the community, resulting in local officers not continuously following up with patients. The National Tuberculosis Information Program (NTIP) for tuberculosis patient data management was being used only at the hospital level. Researchers and the sample group together developed a model for tracking pulmonary tuberculosis patients to ensure continuous treatment and medication adherence until completion, according to the Tuberculosis Division, Department of Disease Control’s principle of “Find cases, Finish with cure” (Tuberculosis Division, Department of Disease Control, 2015).

Additionally, demonstrations were conducted on techniques for communicating with tuberculosis patients to motivate them to adhere to medication regularly. 2) A follow-up plan for pulmonary tuberculosis patients in Nonthaburi Province. A collaborative plan was drawn up involving tuberculosis clinic staff, staff from the Provincial Health Office, district health offices of the six districts, and 16 sub-district health-promoting hospitals that currently have tuberculosis patients under treatment, out of a total of 78 such hospitals in the area. Follow-up of pulmonary tuberculosis patients in Nonthaburi Province involves oversight and information sharing via the NTIP program and a Line group for provincial tuberculosis coordinators. The plan covers five key areas: 1) Patient care—tuberculosis clinic staff are to follow up on treatments and ensure all scheduled check-ups are attended, with cooperation requested from treating physicians to adhere to protocols. Introduction The car rental business has become increasingly popular and attractive among today’s entrepreneurs due to its flexibility and ease of operation. Simply owning one’s own vehicle enables an individual to rent it out to those in need of transportation. Most car rental business owners started with renting out just a few vehicles and gradually expanded into larger-scale car rental businesses, offering a wider variety of vehicles. In fact, according to business analysis statistics, every province in Thailand now has at least one car rental provider. As of June 30, 2022, there were a total of 2,045 active businesses in the car and motorcycle rental sector across Thailand (Department of Business Development, 2022). A car rental business is similar to renting out a house or condominium, but in this business, income is generated by leasing vehicles. The vehicle owner or lessor rents out their own car to individuals who need a vehicle for driving in accordance with their intended purpose. Types of vehicles available for rent include private cars, motorcycles, vans, trucks, and other types of vehicles. Renters are required to pay the rental fee as stipulated in the vehicle rental agreement made jointly with the owner. There are two types of car rental: 1) Operating leases, which involve long-term rentals based on a contract. The renter pays monthly rental fees and is responsible for fuel costs, while the lessor is responsible for maintenance, insurance, and other related expenses. This type of lease is suitable for large-scale operators with a diverse vehicle fleet, and the primary clients are usually private companies, government agencies, or state enterprises. 2) Short-term rentals, where cars are rented out only for a short period—daily, weekly, or monthly. These may include private cars, vans, pickups, or motorcycles. This type of lease suits small-scale operators who wish to rent out their personal vehicles, with customers mainly being general individuals or tourists. Currently, as many groups of tourists visit Thailand and face travel challenges within the area, this type of service has grown popular (Ngern Tid Lor, 2023). The traditional service system in car rental shops involved the following process: when a customer wished to rent a car, the renter was required to present an ID card and a driver’s license to the shop as proof for vehicle rental. After the renter selected a car, the staff would fill out a car rental form and record the return date, storing this information in a file folder. The staff handled payment collection for the rental, and upon return, would check the return date and inspect the vehicle for any damages in order to calculate fines or repair costs if necessary. When new vehicles arrived, the shop purchased cars and made them available for rental in the “new vehicles” section. Clearly, the traditional system relied on manual labor at every step, which led to delays in providing rental service. For instance, issues like recording the return date or handing over the vehicle could be delayed, as a single staff member may oversee multiple rentals, causing confusion. This might result in issues such as incorrect recording of return dates, or failing to remember if a specific customer had previously borrowed a car before renting again.

Ability to Return to Work of Patients with Myocardial Infarction after Cardiac Rehabilitation in Phatthalung Hospital Benchamat Salinpramote1* Abstract Cardiac rehabilitation in patients with myocardial infarction aims to prevent complications and enable patients to perform daily activities. This descriptive research aimed to study the ability to return to work and factors influencing the return to work after cardiac rehabilitation. The sample group consisted of 113 patients with myocardial infarction, aged less than or equal to 65 years, who received treatment at the Cardiac Rehabilitation Center, Department of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation, Phatthalung Hospital, and who were employed prior to the event. Data were collected from medical records and by using the Duke Activity Status Index (DASI - Thai version), the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (Thai HADS), and physical fitness testing by the 6-Minute Walk Test (6MWT). Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, Chi-square test, Fisher exact test, and paired t-test. The results showed that 88.49% of patients were able to return to work. Statistically significant factors (p-value < 0.05) influencing return to work included gender, age, occupation, income, type of treatment, left ventricular ejection fraction, smoking, alcohol consumption, exercise before and after the illness, presence of chest pain after treatment, level of physical activity, and 6-minute walk test results. Therefore, cardiac rehabilitation programs in patients with myocardial infarction should be promoted, along with consideration of other influencing factors, to increase patients’ ability to return to work. Keywords: Ability to return to work, patients with myocardial infarction, cardiac rehabilitation program Introduction Cardiac rehabilitation not only reduces mortality in patients but also helps them recover more quickly, increases confidence in resuming daily activities at home(3-4), and enables individuals to reintegrate happily into society(5-6). The ability to return to previous work is an important indicator of quality of life for patients of working age. Inability to return to work results in lost income, which affects self-care and family care, potentially leading to loss of self-confidence and impacting social participation(7). Literature reviews indicate that patients are able to return to work after heart surgery at rates ranging from 30% to 78%, although some will perform less work than before(4-5). A study at Phramongkutklao Hospital found that after treatment and cardiac rehabilitation, 93.2% of heart disease patients could fully perform activities of daily living, and patients reported that cardiac rehabilitation increased their confidence in carrying out these activities. Currently, cardiovascular disease is a major health problem in Thailand, especially due to the rising incidence of myocardial infarction, which is the leading cause of death within this disease group(1). Treatment for myocardial infarction includes several modalities such as thrombolytic therapy, percutaneous coronary intervention, coronary artery bypass graft surgery, and cardiac rehabilitation as a component of care. Both domestic and international studies have found that cardiac rehabilitation, which consists of exercise, education about risk factors, behavioral modification, and psychological counseling, can prevent complications from prolonged bed rest or lack of movement(2), increase blood circulation, and improve cardiac function. Thus, it reduces recurrence rates and mortality. Approximately 83.8% of patients reported confidence in daily activities and exercise(8). A study by the Cardiac Rehabilitation Department at Ramathibodi Hospital found that 60.7% of post-cardiac surgery patients who received cardiac rehabilitation were able to return to work within an average of 4.6 months, although the sample size was only 56 individuals(9). Another study found that among patients aged not over 65 who underwent surgery and received cardiac rehabilitation, 36.2% were able to return to work, and the study also examined factors influencing work resumption(10). The Rehabilitation Medicine Division of Phatthalung Hospital established the Cardiac Rehabilitation Center for patients with myocardial infarction on July 1, 2018, by providing a cardiac rehabilitation program to patients during their first follow-up visit four weeks post-discharge. The number of patients receiving treatment increased from 29 in 2018 to 107 in 2019. Given that studies on the ability of patients with myocardial infarction to return to work and the factors influencing work resumption after cardiac rehabilitation remain limited, and considering the environmental, social, and cultural differences in the South compared to other regions—which affect ways of life and work characteristics—the researcher is interested in studying the ability of patients with myocardial infarction aged not over 65 years who have received cardiac rehabilitation at the Cardiac Rehabilitation Center, Department of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation, Phatthalung Hospital, as well as related factors. The findings are intended to serve as a guideline for improving and planning future patient care. Objectives 1) To study the ability of patients with myocardial infarction to return to work after cardiac rehabilitation. 2) To study the factors associated with the return to work among patients with heart disease. Other factors included age, income, and anxiety or depression that affected return to work, possibly due to differing social and environmental factors. This contrasts the studies of Simchen E et al.(21) and Kittipol Jitachatarn et al.(10), which found return-to-work rates of 53.10% and 36.20%, respectively, with factors affecting return to work including gender, age, and participation in cardiac rehabilitation. However, in those studies, most patients underwent open heart surgery (76.64% and 58.50%, respectively), which may have resulted in more severe cardiac and thoracic tissue damage and thus a lower return to work rate compared to this study. Moreover, patients with very good physical activity levels according to the DASI (Thai version) and greater improvement on the 6 MWT after rehabilitation were more likely to return to work. This is consistent with the study by Rungtiwa Suriya et al.(22), which found that participation in a cardiac rehabilitation program led to improved physical activity levels as measured by DASI (reaching moderate METs for activities) and increased 6MWT distance, boosting patients' confidence to engage in activity. However, this differs from the study by Kittipol Jitachatarn et al.(10), which found these two factors were not associated with return to work, possibly because both groups had similarly improved abilities and 6MWT distances before and after the rehabilitation program. From this study, factors that had a statistically significant effect on return to work included gender, age, occupation, income, type of treatment, ejection fraction, smoking, alcohol consumption, exercise before and after myocardial infarction, chest pain after treatment, level of physical activity according to DASI (Thai version), and 6MWT before and after rehabilitation. In contrast, body mass index and anxiety or depression did not significantly affect return to work, possibly because both groups (returned to work or not) had similar characteristics, i.e., most had an overweight BMI and few reported anxiety or depression. The study found that males, government officers/company employees, non-smokers, and those with no chest pain after treatment were more likely to return to work, in line with the research of Jiang Z et al.(17) and Mustafah N et al.(18), which found return-to-work rates of 55.90% and 66.70%, respectively, with female gender, light work, non-smoking, and absence of chest pain or recurrent heart disease after discharge as influencing factors. Other relevant factors included education level and comorbidities, such as hyperlipidemia and diabetes, which were not included in this study. This study also found that patients with normal ejection fraction had a higher likelihood of returning to work, consistent with European studies by Reibis R et al.(19) and Todd IC et al.(20), which found return-to-work rates of 91%, similar to this study, with male gender, office work, and normal ejection fraction as significant factors. Positively correlated factors included physical activity level as measured by DASI (Thai version), appropriate exercise before and after the cardiac event, 6MWT performance, type of cardiac treatment, alcohol consumption, gender, smoking, and ejection fraction, whereas negatively correlated factors included occupation and chest discomfort after treatment. Many of these are modifiable or treatable factors, in line with research by Hegwald J et al.(23) and Shephard RJ(24), which found that appropriate exercise after a cardiac event and non-smoking increased return-to-work rates. Recommendations from the research are: 1) Patients with myocardial infarction should receive a cardiac rehabilitation program as soon as they are admitted and be followed up continuously, as this not only improves physical fitness but also increases the rate of return to work. 2) In counseling patients, the identified influencing factors should be referenced in predicting return to work, and positive factors, such as appropriate exercise, moderation in alcohol, and not smoking, should be promoted during patient care, while negative factors should be carefully monitored. 3) Further research should study a larger sample, identify other potentially related factors such as lesion location, number of affected coronary vessels, comorbidities, education level, and family factors, and follow up long-term after cardiac rehabilitation. It is also recommended to compare return-to-work rates in patients who do and do not participate in cardiac rehabilitation programs, as well as the rate of recurrence and patient quality of life.

The benefits of herbal use in disease treatment derive from knowledge passed down from ancestors. Currently, the World Health Organization recognizes the importance of developing herbs for health care (Chanida Mattawangkur, Kwannruen Kawitu, and Suthida Deenu, 2019). When thinking of herbs, people often regard them as only plants or natural products, leading to a sense of safety when choosing to use them. However, improper or incorrect use of herbs has caused harm to users, with clinical reports indicating a rise in adverse reactions, especially among those using herbs as medicines (Mahidol University, 2020). 2. To study the behaviors of individuals using herbs in the management of long COVID after coronavirus 2019 infection among residents of Ang Sila Subdistrict, Chonburi Province. A review of the literature on the use of herbs for managing long COVID, also known as post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection (PASC), shows that symptoms persist beyond the acute phase of COVID-19, which significantly affects individuals’ quality of life. As conventional medical approaches are often insufficient for managing the multitude of symptoms associated with long COVID, there has been growing interest in complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) approaches. Herbal medicine, a major component of CAM, offers a promising avenue for managing long COVID symptoms due to its diverse pharmacological properties and historical use in treating respiratory infections and inflammatory conditions (Nguyen et al., 2021). Several herbs have demonstrated potential efficacy in alleviating long COVID symptoms through immunomodulatory, anti-inflammatory, and antiviral actions. For example, traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) formulations such as Qing-Fei-Pai-Du-Tang (QFPDT) and Yu Ping Feng San (YPFS) have shown promising results in relieving respiratory symptoms and modulating immune responses in COVID-19 patients (Wang et al., 2021). Similarly, herbal extracts such as Andrographis paniculata, Glycyrrhiza glabra, and Panax ginseng possess antiviral properties against coronaviruses and may help alleviate long COVID symptoms by targeting viral replication and inflammation (Elfiky, 2020; Kurokawa et al., 2015). However, while herbs may offer benefits in managing long COVID, caution is warranted due to limited clinical evidence and potential herb-drug interactions. Integrating herbal medicine into the management of long-term illness requires a comprehensive understanding of the pharmacological profiles of individual herbs, appropriate dosing, and possible side effects. Furthermore, collaboration between traditional herbal practitioners and conventional healthcare providers is essential to ensure the safe and effective integration of herbs into treatment protocols (Hu et al., 2021). In summary, herbal medicine is considered a potential complementary approach to managing long COVID symptoms, drawing on its diverse therapeutic properties. Further research examining the efficacy and safety of certain herbs, as well as their integration into conventional treatment regimens, is warranted to provide evidence-based guidance for individuals grappling with the debilitating effects of long COVID. During the COVID-19 pandemic, there was a trend of using herbs to treat Long COVID symptoms. As the participants were already interested in herbs, new information was seen as an option that did not conflict with their own needs. When trying herbal remedies, they tended to focus solely on their benefits to the body rather than any potential harms. Some even believed that daily consumption of herbs could prevent future illnesses. This is in accordance with the PRECEDE-PROCEED Model, cited in the research by Wasana Sithikan (2022), which suggests that an individual's behaviors result from multiple factors: personal, environmental, policy, and online media influences. Personal factors, the interplay of various determinants, affect health behaviors. Behavior change toward health-promoting actions only occurs when there are motivating factors, but such behaviors may not be complete if the individual does not receive essential environmental support. Hence, receiving appropriate environmental factors encourages health-promoting behaviors. Moreover, receiving policy and online media influence, as well as support from close individuals, further motivates the expression of health-promoting behaviors.
